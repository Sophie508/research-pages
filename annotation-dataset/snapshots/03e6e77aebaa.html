Abstract
Background
The sulfur microbial diet (SMD), a dietary pattern linked to sulfur-metabolizing gut bacteria, has been associated with metabolic disorders. However, its role in dementia risk remains unclear.
Methods
This prospective cohort study investigated the association between SMD and dementia risk and examined potential mechanisms. We included 125,714 UK Biobank participants who completed at least two 24-hour dietary recalls. SMD scores were calculated based on food intake associated with sulfur-metabolizing bacteria. The primary outcome was incident dementia, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), vascular dementia (VD), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Secondary outcomes included cognitive function and brain volume changes. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Stratified and mediation analyses were conducted to explore potential population heterogeneity and mechanisms.
Results
Over a median follow-up of 8.58 years, 691 dementia cases occurred. A U-shaped association was observed between SMD scores and the risk of dementia and AD. Compared with the middle quintile, participants in the highest quintile had a 37% higher dementia risk (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.08–1.74) and a 46% higher AD risk (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.00–2.14). No significant association was found for the lowest quintile. Associations were stronger in males and older adults. Diabetes mediated 8% of the total effect of high SMD adherence on dementia risk. No significant associations were observed for VD and FTD. Cognitive function changes were not notable. Higher SMD adherence was linked to reduced thalamic grey matter volume, while lower adherence was associated with increased volume in the precentral and postcentral gyri.
Conclusions
A U-shaped relationship exists between SMD and dementia risk, particularly AD, with potential sex- and age-specific differences. Diabetes may partly mediate this association. SMD may also affect brain volume. These findings suggest a possible role of SMD in the diet-microbiota-gut-brain axis and neurodegeneration.

Introduction
Dementia is a syndrome characterized by progressive cognitive decline, leading to impairments in daily living and occupational functioning [1]. It is among the top ten global causes of death [2], affecting over 57.4 million people worldwide and imposing an annual societal cost of $131.34 billion [3, 4]. Alarmingly, with the rapidly aging population, the number of dementia cases is projected to reach 153 million within the next 25 years [3]. Despite the urgent need for preventive strategies, the exact etiology of dementia remains incompletely understood. Therefore, extensive population-based etiological research is crucial to inform effective interventions.
The diet-microbiota-gut-brain axis has been proposed as a key pathway in dementia pathogenesis. The interaction between dietary intake and gut microbiota composition is well established [5]. Previous causal studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota composition is associated with the risk of dementia and its subtypes, with several sulfur-metabolizing bacteria playing a particularly significant role [6,7,8]. These bacteria produce intestinal hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which, in excess, may diffuse into the bloodstream [9]. H2S exerts biphasic effects on brain health, depending on its concentration [10, 11]. At physiological levels, it acts as a gaseous signaling molecule with neuroprotective properties, whereas at higher concentrations, it becomes neurotoxic. These findings suggest a potential J- or U-shaped association between H2S levels and brain health. Additionally, recent research suggests that H2S produced by sulfur-metabolizing bacteria in mice may disrupt host metabolism [12], potentially contributing to dementia development. However, large-scale population-based evidence remains limited. Furthermore, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms, particularly how sulfur-metabolizing bacteria facilitate the transition from multiple chronic diseases to dementia, are not yet fully elucidated.
A novel dietary pattern, the sulfur microbial diet (SMD), was developed to capture the dietary drivers of sulfur-metabolizing gut bacteria. This pattern was constructed by linking the intake of various foods and the abundance of 43 sulfur-metabolizing gut bacteria through reduced rank regression and stepwise regression [13], which has been independently validated in multiple studies [13,14,15]. SMD is characterized by higher intakes of processed meats, liquor, and low-calorie beverages, and lower intakes of fruit juice, beer, legumes, other vegetables, and sweet & desserts, which in high adherence is thought to promote the growth of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria such as Bilophila wadsworthia and Desulfovibrio desulfuricans. Previous studies have associated higher SMD adherence with increased risk of obesity, nonalcoholic liver disease, and ovarian cancer [16,17,18]. However, its potential impact on dementia risk remains unclear.
Therefore, leveraging data from the UK Biobank, this study aims to examine the prospective association of SMD with incident dementia among participants free of dementia at baseline, as well as changes in cognitive function and brain structure, and explore the potential mechanism.
Method
Study design and participants
This prospective cohort study was conducted using data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale biomedical database that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from England, Wales, and Scotland. Detailed information regarding the UK Biobank has been published elsewhere [19]. In brief, baseline assessments were conducted between 2006 and 2010, followed by four additional 24-hour dietary recalls between 2011 and 2012. Two subsequent imaging visits took place in 2014 and 2019. The UK Biobank continuously tracks participants’ health outcomes through linkage with nationwide hospital admission records and death registries. To reduce random errors from single dietary assessment and minimize reverse causality, the current study included participants who had completed at least two 24-hour dietary recalls and had no diagnosis of dementia at the baseline. The date of the last 24-hour dietary recall was considered the start of follow-up, and participants were followed until November 30, 2020. Additionally, individuals with implausible daily energy intake (female: < 500 or > 3500 kcal; male: < 800 or > 4200 kcal) were excluded. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Fig. 1.
Assessment of sulfur microbial diet
Dietary data were collected using the Oxford WebQ, a validated, self-administered, web-based questionnaire designed for large-scale epidemiological studies [20]. The average daily intake (serving/day) of more than 200 food items was estimated from multiple dietary assessments.
The SMD score was calculated based on previous research [17], incorporating three positively associated food groups (processed meats, liquor, and low-calorie drinks) and five negatively associated food groups (beer, fruit juice, legumes, other vegetables, and sweets or desserts). A higher SMD score indicates greater adherence to SMD and a potentially higher abundance of sulfur-metabolizing microbiota. In this study, the SMD score ranged from − 10.08 to 5.00, with a median of -1.15, and was categorized into five groups based on the following quintile cut-off points: -1.86, -1.35, -0.96, and − 0.50.
To better understand the dietary pattern represented by the SMD score, we examined the trends in food group intakes across SMD quintiles and assessed the relative contribution of each food group to the total SMD score within each quintile. As shown in Fig. 2, daily intakes of all eight food groups were significantly correlated with SMD score quintiles. The relative contribution of each food group also varied, with other vegetables being the primary contributor in most quintiles, except in the highest quintile, where processed meats contributed the most.
Assessment of incident dementia
Incident dementia was identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th revision (ICD-9/10) codes (see Supplementary Material 1: Reference for calculation and definition). Dementia diagnoses were obtained through linkage with national hospital admission and mortality records and were confirmed by professional clinicians. Subtypes of dementia included Alzheimer’s disease, vascular dementia, and frontotemporal dementia. For this study, incident dementia was defined as new diagnoses occurring after the last 24-hour dietary recall.
Assessment of cognitive decline
Cognitive function was assessed at baseline and during imaging visits through five validated tests: prospective memory (binary outcome: correct/incorrect), reaction time (continuous variable; higher values indicate poorer performance), visual memory, numeric memory, and reasoning (count variables; higher scores indicate better performance).
A composite cognitive function score was generated using the method of Factor Analysis of Mixed Data [21]. The first principal dimension, positively correlated with cognitive performance, explained 30.5%, 31.3%, and 32.3% of the variance at baseline, the first imaging visit, and the second imaging visit, respectively. Cognitive decline was defined as the difference between the cognitive scores at each follow-up visit and baseline.
Assessment of brain volume change
The brain volume was assessed using T1-weighted structural MRI (Siemens Skyra 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil) during both imaging visits. Brain volume measures included total brain volumes (whole brain, white matter, grey matter), subcortical volumes, and regional grey matter volumes. Assessment of covariates
Potential confounders were selected on existing evidence [23,24,25], encompassing inherent, flexible, and clinical factors. Inherent factors included sex, age, ethnicity, family history of dementia, and ApoE ε4 carrier status. Flexible factors included body mass index (BMI), Townsend deprivation index (TDI), educational qualifications, smoking status, physical activity, social isolation, air pollution exposure, serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and daily energy intake. Alcohol consumption was not separately adjusted, as liquor and beer servings were already included in the SMD calculation. Clinical factors included hearing loss, visual impairment, diabetes, hypertension, traumatic brain injury (TBI), depression, and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Socioeconomic and lifestyle data were collected through structured questionnaires, while physical and biochemical measurements were obtained at the assessment centers. Air pollution exposure was estimated using a Land Use Regression model.
Specifically, dementia family history was defined as having any first-degree relatives (parents and siblings) with dementia. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m). TDI is a comprehensive index reflecting relative privation, assessed based on unemployment, non-car ownership, non-home ownership, and household overcrowding [26]. Physical activity was categorized as low, moderate, and high levels according to the guidelines of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire [27]. Social isolation was identified through three items: participants who reported “living alone”, “less than one friend or family visit per month”, or “not participating in any social activities” would receive 1 point each, and levels were further classified into “least” (0 points), “moderate” (1 point), and “most” (2–3 points) [28]. As no comprehensive and dynamical air pollution indicator was available in the UK Biobank, NO2 and PM2.5 levels according to the residential address in 2010 were utilized to evaluate baseline air pollution. Baseline clinical factors included self-reported cases and those diagnosed before the last 24-hour diet recall according to ICD-9/10.
Assessment of mediators
Five chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, depression, IBD, and obesity) were examined as potential mediators linking SMD to dementia. These conditions included both pre-existing cases at baseline and newly diagnosed cases during follow-up.
Additionally, mediation analyses explored the potential role of 14 serum biomarkers, including lipid profiles (apolipoprotein A, apolipoprotein B, total cholesterol, lipoprotein A, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides), inflammatory/metabolic markers (C-reactive protein, glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin type A1c, insulin-like growth factor 1), and hormones (sex hormone-binding globulin, oestradiol, and testosterone).
Statistical analysis
Participants were categorized into five groups according to quintiles of the SMD score (Q1 to Q5). Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test, while continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and tested using analysis of variance. Missing values for covariates were imputed using multiple imputation with the random forest method. The proportions of missing data for each variable are summarized in Supplementary Material 1: Table S1.
Four regression models were developed to progressively adjust for potential confounders. Model A was the unadjusted model. Model B adjusted for inherent factors (sex, age, ethnicity, family history of dementia, and ApoE ε4 carrier status). Model C further adjusted for baseline flexible factors (BMI, TDI, educational qualifications, smoking status, physical activity, social isolation, NO2 and PM2.5 exposure, serum LDL-C level, and energy intake). Model D was the fully adjusted model, which additionally included baseline clinical factors (hearing loss, diabetes, hypertension, TBI, depression, visual impairment, and IBD). Given the potential biphasic effects of H2S on neurocognitive outcomes, the Q3 level was selected as the reference group to assess both lower and higher exposures relative to a moderate SMD adherence.
The primary analysis investigated the association between SMD score quintiles and incident dementia using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated for each quintile. Based on the pattern observed in the categorical analysis, a restricted cubic spline function with three knots was employed to assess potential nonlinear dose-response relationships based on Model D. The non-linearity was tested using a likelihood ratio test comparing the spline model to a linear model.
To evaluate the robustness of the main findings and to address potential concerns regarding reverse causation and misclassification bias, several sensitivity analyses were performed based on Model D: (A) re-estimating the association using stepwise selection; (B) including participants with only one 24-hour dietary recall; (C) conducting a competing risk analysis to account for death as a competing event; and (D) excluding dementia cases whose follow-up duration was less than 365 days.
Further analyses were conducted based on Model D. Stratified analyses by sex, age, BMI, and ApoE ε4 carrier status were conducted to assess potential effect heterogeneity; interaction effects were evaluated using a likelihood ratio test comparing models with and without the cross-product terms between SMD score levels and the stratifying variables. Causal mediation analyses employing Cox regression (Supplementary Material 1: Reference for calculation and definition) were conducted to estimate the natural indirect effects and the proportion of the association mediated by chronic diseases and serum biomarkers. Linear regression analyses were performed to examine the associations of SMD score quintiles and changes in cognitive function and brain volume.
All statistical analyses were carried out using R (version 4.2.3). Packages “mice”, “survival”, “rms”, “CMAverse”, “tidycmprsk” were employed for imputation, Cox proportional hazards regression, restricted cubic spline, mediation analyses, and competing risk model, respectively. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Baseline characteristics
Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the 125,714 participants, categorized by quintiles of SMD score. The proportions of males, current smokers, and participants with hearing loss, diabetes, hypertension, and depression exhibited a V-shaped distribution across SMD score quintiles. A similar pattern was observed for the mean BMI, TDI, NO2 exposure, and PM2.5 exposure. In contrast, an inverse trend was noted for participants with a family history of dementia and those with a college or university qualification, as well as for the mean serum LDL-C level. The proportion of participants with high social isolation levels and IBD increased with higher SMD quintiles, whereas those with high physical activity levels, along with the mean age and energy intake, showed a decreasing trend. Statistically significant differences were also found in ethnicity among the groups, with observed lower proportions of Caucasian in Q4 and Q5 groups. However, no significant differences (p > 0.05) were observed for ApoE ε4 carrier status, visual impairment, and TBI.
Associations between SMD score levels and dementia
After a median follow-up of 8.58 years (interquartile range: 8.51–9.01 years), a total of 691 incident dementia cases were recorded, comprising 275 cases of AD, 111 cases of VD, 25 cases of FTD, and 302 unclassified cases.
As shown in Table 2, in Model B, the highest quintile (Q5) of SMD scores was significantly associated with an increased risk of dementia and AD. This association remained significant, albeit slightly attenuated, after further adjusting for flexible factors (Model C) and clinical factors (Model D). In the fully adjusted Model D, compared with participants in Q3, those in Q5 had a 37% increased risk of dementia (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.08–1.74) and a 46% increased risk of AD (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.00–2.14). However, no significant association was observed between the highest SMD score level and the risk of VD (HR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.60–1.93) or FTD (HR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.02–1.49).
Given the observed nonlinear association of dementia and AD risks with SMD scores in the Cox regression analysis, a restricted cubic spline function based on Model D was utilized. Fig. 3 illustrates a U-shaped association between SMD scores and risks of dementia and AD (p for non-linearity = 0.004 and 0.025, respectively). No significant association was observed between SMD scores and the risk of VD or FTD (p = 0.493 and 0.111, respectively).
Stratified analyses by sex, age, BMI, and ApoE ε4 carrier status
Fig. 4 displays the results of stratified analyses examining the associations between SMD score levels with the incidence of dementia, AD, VD, and FTD across different categories of sex, age, BMI, and ApoE ε4 carrier status. The number of participants and cases within each stratum is presented in Supplementary Material 1: Table S2.
Significant interactions were observed between sex and SMD score levels in relation to dementia and AD risk (p for interaction = 0.034 and 0.019, respectively). Specifically, the associations of both low (Q1 and Q2), and high (Q5) SMD scores with increased risks of dementia and AD were pronounced and stronger in males compared to females. Furthermore, there were significant interactions between age and SMD score levels in relation to the risks of dementia and AD (p for interaction = 0.009 and 0.005, respectively). The highest SMD score quintile was significantly associated with an increased risk of dementia and AD in individuals aged 65 years and older, whereas no significant association was found in those younger than 65. Additionally, BMI showed a significant interaction with SMD scores in relation to VD risk (p for interaction < 0.001). A lower VD risk was observed among the Q2 group with a BMI < 25 kg/m2, whereas the Q5 level of SMD scores was significantly associated with an increased risk of VD among participants with a BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m2. No significant association was observed between SMD score levels and VD risk among people with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Moreover, among participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, both Q1 and Q4 SMD score levels were significantly correlated with increased FTD risk; however, no significant interaction was detected between BMI and SMD scores on FTD risk. No significant interactions were observed between ApoE ε4 carrier status and SMD score levels in relation to dementia or its three subtypes.
Mediation effects
The mediation analysis results of five dementia-related chronic diseases and 14 serum biomarkers are presented in Supplementary Material 1: Table S3. Diabetes exhibited a significant mediating effect in the association between the highest quintile level of SMD scores and dementia, with a proportion mediated of 8% (Fig. 5). However, no other significant mediators were identified in the mediation analyses of the other chronic diseases or in the analyses of 14 serum biomarkers.
Associations between SMD score levels and secondary outcomes
Secondary outcomes included changes of cognitive function and brain volume. Among the initial study participants, 2,040 and 408 individuals underwent cognitive function assessments during the two subsequent imaging median visits, with median follow-up times of 9.11 and 10.21 years, respectively. Additionally, 1,391 individuals had complete brain volume data for both imaging sessions, with a median interval of 2.22 years.
No statistically significant associations (p > 0.05) were observed between SMD score levels and changes in cognitive function (Supplementary Material 1: Table S4).
Regarding brain structural changes, several statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) were observed (Supplementary Material 1: Table S5). When using Q3 level as the reference, individuals in Q2 exhibited a decrease in putamen volume, while those in Q4 and Q5 showed a reduction in thalamus grey matter volume. Conversely, individuals in Q4 demonstrated an increase in inferior temporal gyrus grey matter volume, and those in Q5 exhibited an increase in postcentral and precentral gyrus grey matter volumes.
Sensitivity analysis results
As shown in Supplementary Material 1: Table S6, the results remained robust across four analyses using different methods or datasets. This suggests that the results were not materially affected by potential exposure misclassification or reverse causation, further supporting the robustness of our findings. Notably, when participants who completed only one 24-hour dietary recall were included, the lowest (Q1) SMD score level was significantly associated with an increased risk of dementia, aligning with the findings from the restricted cubic spline analysis.
Discussion
In this study, we investigated the prospective association between SMD scores and the risk of dementia and its three subtypes within a large-scale population-based cohort. Our findings revealed a U-shaped relationship between SMD scores and incident dementia and AD, with a high adherence to the SMD being linked to a robust increased likelihood of dementia and AD, independent of potential confounders. The associations were more pronounced in males and older individuals. Additionally, high adherence to SMD was linked to an increased risk of VD among overweight individuals. Diabetes partially mediated the relationship between high SMD adherence and dementia risk. Furthermore, different levels of SMD scores were correlated with volumetric changes in specific brain regions.
The impact of single food groups involved within SMD on dementia risk has been widely studied. Prior research has shown that higher intake of processed meat, excessive alcohol consumption, and artificial sweetened beverages increase the risk of dementia and AD [29,30,31], whereas higher consumption of vegetables, legumes, and pure fruit/vegetable juices is associated with a lower risk [31,32,33]. However, these findings are often attributed to the effect of specific food components such as nutrients and bioactive compounds, neglecting the complex interactions among various foods and the role of gut microbiota. Our study firstly reported the role of a gut microbiota related diet pattern in the development of dementia and AD, offering an insight into the role of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria on brain health.
Diet plays a critical role in shaping the composition of the gut microbiota [5], and varying degrees of adherence to SMD may influence the proliferation of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria and the subsequent production of H2S [13]. Low concentrations of H2S in the gut have been shown to support mucosal repair [34], whereas excessive H2S can compromise intestinal barrier integrity [35], allowing unmetabolized H2S to enter the circulation [9, 36]. While physiological levels of circulating H2S exert neuroprotective properties [10], reductions in endogenous H2S have been associated with an increased risk of AD [37]. Conversely, excessive levels of H2S may damage neuronal mitochondria and lysosomes, contributing to neurodegeneration [11]. In addition, elevated H2S levels in the gut can disrupt metabolic pathways, reduce glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion, and promote systemic inflammation [12], all of which are implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegeneration [38,39,40]. A reduction in GLP-1 may also lead to the development of diabetes [41], and diabetes itself is a known risk factor for dementia through mechanisms such as insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation [42], potentially explaining our findings from the mediation analyses. Furthermore, previous Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated that both decreases and increases in the abundance of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria can elevate the risk of dementia [6,7,8]. Together, these findings suggest that sulfur metabolism may exert bidirectional effects on the nervous system. In line with these existing mechanisms, our study demonstrated a U-shaped association of SMD score with dementia and AD. However, given the observational and exploratory nature of our analysis, this association should be interpreted with caution. Further research is needed to replicate this finding and investigate the underlying biological pathways. Notably, the majority of the mechanistic evidence regarding sulfur metabolism and neurodegeneration comes from animal studies, which limits the generalizability of these findings to humans. Therefore, additional mechanistic studies in human populations are warranted to strengthen causal inference. We did not find any significant associations between SMD and the risk of VD or FTD, possibly due to a relatively small number of incident cases.
In the present study, we also observed that the associations between SMD and dementia risk varied across different subpopulations. Although females are generally at a higher risk for all-cause dementia and AD [43], our stratified analysis revealed stronger associations between SMD and the risk of dementia and AD in males. This sex difference is unlikely to be explained by sex hormones, as our mediation analysis did not identify a significant role for them. Instead, diabetes, more prevalent in males [44], emerged as a partial mediator in the relationship between SMD and dementia, potentially accounting for this observed sex disparity. Additionally, a high SMD adherence was associated with an increased risk of dementia and AD among individuals aged 65 years or older, but this association was not observed in those under 65 years, suggesting an increasing sensitivity to SMD influences with advancing age. This finding may be attributed to age-related declines in immune function and metabolic capacity [45]. Furthermore, the interaction between SMD and BMI in relation to VD risk underscores the complex interplay between diet, metabolism, and neurodegeneration. The second quintile of SMD scores was negatively associated with VD risk among non-overweight individuals, whereas the detrimental effects of the highest quintile appeared to be amplified in overweight ones. One possible explanation is that higher body weight is associated with low-grade systemic inflammation and impaired gut barrier function [46], which may amplify the adverse effects of high SMD adherence on dementia development. No significant association was found in obese individuals, potentially because obesity is associated with high SMD adherence [16], which may overshadow the impact of SMD on VD risk. In contrast, we found no significant differences in the association between SMD and dementia risk when stratified by ApoE ε4 carrier status, indicating that the effects of SMD on dementia risk may be independent of genetic susceptibility.
For secondary outcomes, we did not observe any statistically significant changes in cognitive function between baseline and the two imaging visits, likely due to a short time interval and limited sample size. These limitations constrain the interpretability of the results. However, in exploratory analyses of regional grey matter volumes, we identified statistically significant spatial differences. Specifically, participants with high SMD adherence showed significantly decreased grey matter volume in the thalamus, a region closely associated with cognitive function [47]. Conversely, individuals with low SMD adherence showed increased grey matter volume in the postcentral and precentral gyri, regions responsible for motor control and sensory processing [48]; however, the clinical relevance of these observations remains unclear. Mechanistically, as a gaseous signaling molecule, H2S has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic effects within the nervous system [49]. On the other hand, acute exposure to H2S has been shown to induce lesions in brain areas such as the thalamus [50]. In addition, GLP-1 receptors are highly expressed in brain regions involved in memory and learning, thus a reduction in GLP-1 levels may impair receptor activity and subsequently compromise cerebral structure [51]. Given that abnormal structural brain changes often precede the clinical onset of dementia [52], these findings support the need for further research.
To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between SMD and dementia risk, offering several strengths. First, the large-scale population-based cohort design provided robust statistical power and prospective insights. Second, the use of multiple dietary recalls enhanced the reliability of dietary assessments. Third, comprehensive adjustment for a wide range of sociodemographic, genetic, and clinical covariates minimized potential confounding. Finally, multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our findings.
However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, our study population primarily comprised British residents, limiting generalizability to populations with different genetic and dietary backgrounds. Second, although the age-stratified analyses supported the robustness of the associations between SMD scores and dementia as well as AD, the relatively short follow-up duration and the younger baseline age of participants may have contributed to the limited number of dementia cases, potentially affecting statistical power, particularly for VD and FTD analyses. Third, reliance on 24-hour dietary recalls without longitudinal dietary tracking might introduce potential recall and reporting biases. Fourth, participants who completed the imaging visits were likely healthier or more health-conscious, while those with poorer health may have been less able to attend, potentially introducing selection bias. Fifth, covariates in the regression models, such as BMI, TDI, and air pollution, were measured at baseline or fixed timepoints and thus may not reflect their dynamic changes over the follow-up period. Sixth, the relatively small sample size of imaging analyses constrained our ability to explore associations between SMD and secondary outcomes. Seventh, despite adjusting for a wide range of covariates, residual confounding remains difficult to fully eliminate. Lastly, although the SMD score has been studied and applied in prior research, we were unable to externally validate this indicator in this study due to the absence of direct measurements of gut microbiome composition or circulating H2S levels, which limits mechanistic interpretation.
Conclusion
Our prospective cohort study identified a U-shaped association between SMD and the risk of incident dementia and AD, with variations by sex and age. Diabetes was identified as a partial mediator in the relationship between SMD and dementia risk. These hypothesis-generating findings suggest a possible role of sulfur-metabolizing bacteria in the diet-microbiota-gut-brain axis and neurodegeneration, although causal relationships cannot be inferred. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings in ethnically and geographically diverse populations and to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms.
