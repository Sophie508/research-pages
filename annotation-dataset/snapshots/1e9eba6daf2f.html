9 Things People With Kidney Disease Need to Know About Hyperkalemia
High potassium levels in your blood can go veritably undetected…until trouble starts in a hurry.
Hyperkalemia, the medical term for too much potassium in the body, is common in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Hyperkalemia frequency is as high as 40% to 50% in people with CKD, compared to 2% to 3% in the general population, according to the National Kidney Foundation (NKF). The kidneys are like the body’s washing machine—they remove all the toxins and waste in our blood via urine, while holding on to good stuff like protein and balancing potassium levels. When these two fist-sized organs in the lower back start to slack, potassium can build up in the bloodstream—and that’s a problem.
Hyperkalemia Is Serious
“Having the right balance of potassium in the body is critical because it generates electrical activity in the cells that make the heart pump and the muscles move,” says Donald Molony, M.D., a nephrologist with UTHealth Houston McGovern Medical School and Memorial Hermann-Texas Medical Center in Houston, TX. When the kidneys’ function drops to 60% or less (CKD stage 3 or worse), they can’t rid the body of excess potassium like they used to, resulting in hyperkalemia, which can cause muscle weakness, numbness, tingling, nausea or vomiting, heart palpitations, an irregular heart beat (arrhythmia), shortness of breath, chest pain, and heart attack.
Some People Are at Higher Risk
Even among people with CKD, certain groups are more vulnerable. “Hyperkalemia is more common in individuals living with both CKD and diabetes, as well as people with CKD who may be taking certain blood pressure medications,” says Sylvia Rosas, M.D., a nephrologist and epidemiologist at the Joslin Diabetes Center in Boston, MA, and president of the NKF Board of Directors. “Other risk factors include higher potassium intake, lower serum bicarbonate levels (which cause too much acid in the body, known as metabolic acidosis), lower kidney function, and taking immunosuppressant drugs to avoid rejection of a kidney transplant.”
Too Much Potassium Can Affect Your Heart
Your cardiovascular system takes the brunt of the damage from hyperkalemia. That’s because the electrical signaling of potassium tells your myocardium muscle to pump blood into your heart and through your body. Too much of it causes your heart to beat irregularly. “We can see classic changes in the electrical pattern on an EKG that’s a precursor,” notes Dr. Molony. The risk for arrhythmias (an irregular heartbeat) such as ventricular fibrillation—the most frequent cause of sudden cardiac death—is what makes hyperkalemia life-threatening, says Dr. Rosas. Get medical help if you experience chest pain, shortness of breath, and heart palpitations.
You Can’t NOT Eat Potassium
Name a food or beverage and it’s got potassium in it. Tomatoes, avocados, carrots, spinach, chicken, salmon, nuts, and yogurt are considered high potassium foods (more than 200 mg), while apples, grapes, pineapple, asparagus, eggplant, kale, noodles, pasta, and rice are low potassium (less than 200 mg). This guide from the NKF can help you carefully balance your diet, as can working with a dedicated renal dietitian (find one at eatright.org). The recommended daily intake of potassium is 2,600 milligrams for women and 3,400 mg for men, according to the National Institutes of Health, but you may need less.
Love high potassium potatoes? Peel them (the skin is loaded with potassium), then boil them to help reduce the content even more. A soaking process known as leaching can help reduce potassium levels in veggies. Steer clear of low-salt packaged foods and salt substitutes too. “Salt substitutes are like pouring potassium into your body,” notes Dr. Molony. And watch out for hidden potassium-containing preservatives in frozen meals and canned goods. If you’re cooking canned vegetables or canned beans, cut down the potassium content by first draining then rinsing them thoroughly.
Symptoms of Hyperkalemia Can Be Silent
In fact, they almost always are. If you’re experiencing noticeable signs—which may include muscle weakness, numbness, tingling, heart palpitations, and nausea or vomiting—you’re in the danger zone. “Hyperkalemia is silent until it’s severe, so you don’t want to get to the point that you have symptoms,” notes Charuhas Thakar, M.D., division chief nephrology and hypertension at the University of Cincinnati College of Medicine in Ohio. If they do occur, symptoms tend to be mild and develop over weeks or months. For this reason, people with other risk factors (diabetes, hypertension) will be monitored more frequently, as well as those in the later stages of CKD.
A Simple Blood Test Can Diagnose It
Since the goal is to prevent hyperkalemia before it gets serious enough for symptoms develop, expect your nephrologist to monitor your potassium levels as part of your usual bloodwork every few months, says Dr. Thakar. According to the NKF, a safe level of potassium is 3.5 to 5 milliequivalents per liter of blood (mEq/L). Over 5.1 is a cause for concern, and a value of 6 or greater is the danger zone for severe hyperkalemia, which requires immediate medical care. (Conversely, a 2.5 to 3.4 is also a cause for concern, and below 2.5 is the danger zone for severe low potassium, or hypokalemia.)
Treatment Options Are Improving
Hyperkalemia can be managed with a variety of treatment options depending on the cause, including adjusting or eliminating medications or supplements, notes Dr. Rosas. Diuretics, also known as water pills, may be recommended to pass extra potassium through urine, or potassium binders, which attach extra potassium to stool. Two relatively new potassium-binding agents have shown promising results: Valtessa (patiromer) and Lokelma (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) have been found in clinical studies to reduce potassium levels and keep them in a normal range. Both agents come in a powder form that you mix with a liquid and take daily.
Dialysis Is a Last Resort
Hyperkalemia is more likely to occur in the later stages of kidney failure, when many patients may already be on dialysis, a procedure that cleans the blood for the kidneys. Still, more than half of people living with CKD who are not yet on dialysis develop hyperkalemia, according to NKF. In severe cases when patients may not be responding to other treatments, dialysis may be necessary to stabilize potassium levels, notes Dr. Thaker. But don’t flip out if your doctor suggests it: If kidney function doesn’t worsen as a result of the episode, dialysis may only be needed temporarily.
