Historical background1 
The school meal programme (hereafter SMP) in the Republic of Korea (hereafter Korea) began 
in 1953, immediately following the Korean War (1950-1953), as an emergency response to 
address pervasive child undernutrition. Over the two decades between 1950s-1960s, Korea 
received substantial support from international organizations such as UNICEF, USAID, and 
CARE. As international food aid began to decline from the mid-1960s and 19722, the Korean 
government initiated a transition toward a self-reliant SMP, funded by public budgets and 
beneficiary contributions. In 1981, the enactment of the School Meals Act established the 
institutional foundation for the school meals system still ongoing today. 
Rising female workforce engagement and growing parental demand in the 1990s drove the 
SMP's fast expansion. This momentum was further reinforced by political commitment, 
notably when the programme was adopted as a key pledge in presidential campaigns. As a 
result, its coverage, which had reached only about 12% of elementary schools in the early 
1990s, extended to most of the elementary, middle, and high schools by 2003. By 2012, school 
meals were provided universally in all schools nationwide. In particular, since 2003, policies 
have increasingly focused on improving the quality of school meals. Civil society played a 
central role in advocating for universal free school meals, and despite political and social 
controversy, the free school meal programme began expanding nationwide in 2011. Overtime, 
it came to be recognised as part of universal welfare. As of 2021, all students in elementary, 
middle, high, and special schools in Korea receive free meals, with coverage extended to 
kindergartens in 2022.

Design and implementation of the school meal 
programmes  
 
Description  
Korea SMP aims to support students’ healthy development and enhance national dietary 
habits. Meals are served at lunchtime on school days, based on national nutrition standards 
and the traditional Korean meal structure, which includes rice, soup, kimchi and various 
dishes. As of 2023, the SMP is implemented in 100% of schools nationwide, with nearly full 
student participation. A universal free meal policy has been in place since 2011 and now 
covers all elementary, middle, high, and special education schools. By law, the School Meals 
Act sets standards for food quality and hygiene. It also strongly encourages the use of public 
procurement and electronic contracting to ensure the supply of safe, high-quality, locally 
produced ingredients. The cost of programme is shared by the Offices of Education and local 
governments10, with cost-sharing ratios varying by region. 
 
In term of administrative structure, Korea’s SMP is managed through a coordinated system 
involving the central government, local authorities, and individual schools.  
• At the national level, the Ministry of Education serves as the lead agency, while other 
ministries support with sector-specific responsibilities.  
• At the local level, the 17 Metropolitan and Provincial Offices of Education and local 
governments develop and implement plans tailored to local needs. 
• At the school level, principals manage the programme, while a nutrition teacher (or 
dietitian) is appointed in each school to take exclusive responsibility for school meals. 
The School Governance Committee reviews key issues related to the programme and 
convenes a School Meal Services Committee, which is composed of the nutrition 
teacher (or dietitian), faculty members, and parents (Table 4). 
 
Implementation 
SMP is primarily self-managed under the responsibilities of school principals. When kitchen 
facilities are unavailable, such as during renovations or construction, specific tasks (e.g., staff 
hiring, meal preparation) may be outsourced to external contractors. Off-site preparation and 
transportation to schools are permitted only temporarily in unavoidable cases, such as natural 
disasters or safety concerns. In such cases, the principal is required to report the duration and 
reason for the contract to the regional Office of Education. Contractors must comply with 
hygiene and nutrition standards set by relevant laws and fulfil financial and legal obligations. 
As of 2023, 97.9% of schools (11,783 out of 12,038) operated self-managed meal programmes, 
while 2.1% (255) used contract-managed services.11

Objectives  
The primary goals of the SMP are to support students’ healthy physical and mental 
development and to improve national dietary habits. The programme is also closely aligned 
with national priorities, such as encouraging domestic agricultural consumption and 
strengthening food security.  
•       Health and development: School meals are required to meet students’ nutritional needs 
for healthy growth. The School Meals Act sets standards to ensure that all schools use 
safe, high-quality ingredients and provide a balanced diet. 
•     Dietary habit improvement: Nutrition education is promoted to help students develop 
healthy eating habits and preserve traditional food culture. Schools are encouraged to 
provide dietary education and nutrition counselling to prevent nutrition-related issues. 
•   Food security: As part of a national strategy to protect domestic agriculture, the 
government promotes the use of locally produced ingredients in school meals. This 
contributes to the stability of agricultural household incomes and strengthens national 
food security.13 
 
Coverage and targeting   
The SMP covers all levels of free education in Korea, including kindergartens, elementary, 
middle, high, and special education schools.14 However, private kindergartens with fewer 
than 50 children are excluded from the programme. 
As of 2023, nearly all students in eligible schools are provided with school meals, and such 
coverage is consistent across all school levels from elementary to high school. The coverage 
also indicates equitable implementation of the SMP across metropolitan and provincial 
regions in Korea (Annex 2). 
To ensure that all students have access to school meals regardless of income level, the Korean 
government has implemented a universal free school meal policy since 2011. The cost of 
meals is fully covered by public budget, and beneficiaries are not required to pay any fees. In 
line with this inclusive approach, students from low-income families15 who attend institutions 
not eligible for school meals (e.g., lifelong education institutions16) receive separate subsidies 
equivalent to the standard school meal cost. In Seoul, this group accounts for approximately 
0.1% of all students.17 
 
Meal type 
School meal menus follow the traditional Korean meal structure, typically consisting of rice, 
soup, and three to four dishes for a balanced diet. Meals are usually served on a tray with 
rice, soup, kimchi, a main dish (e.g., meat, fish, eggs, or legumes), and a side dish (e.g., 
vegetables). Rice may be replaced by noodles, and if the noodles are served in soup, no

food items not suitable for on-site cooking (e.g., dairy, fruits, baked goods, sauces, kimchi), 
pre-packed products may be served upon the School Governance Committee’s approval. 
 
Nutritional norms 
The School Meals Act sets Nutritional Standards for School Meals to specify the required 
daily nutrient intake for school lunches, considering students’ gender and age. Schools are 
encouraged to ensure students consume a balanced variety of nutrients in sufficient amounts. 
Each school’s nutrition teacher (or dietitian) should plan menus in accordance with the 
standards, so that the average daily nutrient intake for consecutive 5-day school week every 
season meets the reference level. While the primary goal is to provide the recommended 
nutrient intake (RNI) for each nutrient, flexibility can be allowed within an acceptable range, 
considering the individual health, physical activities and variation across regions (Annex 4). 
When nutrition teachers (or dietitians) design menus, they must consider the following to 
ensure diverse flavours, textures, nutrients, and cultural exposure for students: 
•      Preserve and promote traditional food culture. 
•      Use a variety of food groups (e.g., grains and starches, animal and plant proteins). 
•      Avoid excessive use of salt, fats, added sugars, and food additives. 
•      Prioritize natural and seasonal ingredients whenever possible. 
•      Apply diverse cooking methods. 
 
Nutrition teachers and dietitians 
Most of the schools with meals service facilities have one nutrition teacher (or dietitian), one 
cook, and cooking assistants. Some boarding schools that provide breakfast and dinner may 
have more than one nutrition teacher (or dietitian).  Dietitians are licensed professionals who 
majored in food science or nutrition and passed a national certification examination. Nutrition 
teachers are licensed dietitians who have completed a teacher training program (either 
through an undergraduate teaching curriculum or a graduate program in nutrition education) 
and passed the national teacher qualification examination. Unlike dietitians, nutrition 
teachers are authorized to provide health and nutrition education. Cooks are certified 
through a cooking practical examination. Each cook is assisted by 1 to 13 cooking assistants, 
depending on the number of students served. As of 2023, 10,758 out of 12,038 schools 
providing meals operate their own kitchens, staffed by 11,226 nutrition teachers (or

Food procurement  
Ensuring ingredient quality - The government promotes the use of safe, high-quality 
ingredients to improve school meal quality and increase student satisfaction. It provides food 
category-specific guidelines and financial support to help schools purchase better ingredients. 
1. Quality control standards by food category: The School Meals Act sets recommend 
quality control standards for each food category. When purchasing ingredients, schools 
should prioritise domestic products and check the origin, freshness, and relevant 
certifications, such as environment-friendly labels, Good Agricultural Practices (GAP), 
and Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point (HACCP) in accordance with these 
standards. If certain ingredients are in short supply, schools may substitute them with 
approved alternatives, with the consent of the School Governance Committee. 
However, the use of imported products should be minimized (Annex 5). 
2. Financial support for Good Food Ingredients: The Offices of Education and local 
governments provide subsidies to schools for purchasing certified safe and high-quality 
ingredients, such as non-GMO, environment-friendly, GAP, and HACCP-certified 
products. The amount of support and eligible categories vary depending on the region 
and school level (Annex 6). 
 
Operating procurement and distribution system - To ensure food safety, promote 
transparency in procurement, and support local agriculture, food ingredients are sourced 
through fair procedures and stable local supply channels. 
3. Public procurement through School Meal Service Support Centres (SMSSC): Local 
governments operate SMSSC to manage the entire supply chain of environment-
friendly, locally produced food ingredients. These centres serve as public procurement 
and logistics hubs, ensuring both stable supply and efficient distribution. The functions 
and basic operations of SMSSC are summarized in Annex 7 and 8, and their priorities 
and operational systems may vary by region. Their main roles are: 
•    Simplifying distribution and ensuring quality: Environment-friendly ingredients 
tend to have shorter shelf lives and therefore require fast and reliable logistics, as 
well as quality control. The SMSSC support rapid delivery and conduct safety 
inspections to maintain quality standards. 
•      Promoting local agriculture: The SMSP serve as public platforms that connect local 
farms with schools. They improve school meal quality by providing fresh, locally 
produced ingredients and strengthen the regional economy by establishing a stable 
base for local consumption. They also support sustainable local food systems by 
reducing dependence on imported products. 
•    Ensuring transparent procurement: Since school meals are publicly funded, it is 
essential to ensure fairness and transparency in the procurement procedure. The 
centres prevent collusion and corruption with private vendors by managing joint 
purchasing and supplier oversight.

Legal framework and public policy evolution23 
• 
1981: Enactment of the School Meals Act, defining eligible institutions, facility 
standards, staff placement, and cost-sharing with families. 
• 
1990s: Introduction of contract-managed system to expand coverage; publication of 
HACCP-based hygiene guidelines. 
• 
2000s: Major revision of the Act to improve quality; introduction of the nutrition 
teacher system; legal basis for subsidies for high-quality local ingredients; 
implementation of a food safety accountability system.24 
• 
2006: After a large-scale food poisoning incident, full revision of the Act; reinforcement 
of the self-managed system and introduction of food quality control standards. 
• 
2009, 2013: Amendments of the Act mandating ingredient origin reviews and allergen 
information disclosure. 
• 
2011: Nationwide implementation of free school meals as a universal welfare policy, 
promoting eco-friendly, locally sourced ingredients. (Achieved 100% coverage in 
elementary, middle, and high schools by 2021; introduced in kindergartens in 2022) 
• 
1990, 1994, 1997, 2007, 2021: Nutritional standards revised to reflect updated research 
on student growth and dietary needs. 
Costs of implementation 
School meal expenses are categorized into three main areas: a) food ingredient costs, b) 
operating costs, and c) facility and equipment costs. Operating costs are further divided into 
labor costs, facility maintenance costs, and other costs such as fuel and supplies. 
Food ingredient costs account for the largest share of total expenditure at 52.5%, followed by 
operating costs at 40.5%, and facility and equipment costs at 7.0%. Among operating costs, 
the labor costs account for the majority (34.6%) (Table 5).

Monitoring and evaluation 
Hygiene and safety inspection: Municipal Offices of Education conduct hygiene and safety 
inspections at individual schools twice a year, based on the School Meals Hygiene 
Management Guidelines developed by the Ministry of Education (5th edition, 2021). The 
guidelines apply the principles of the national HACCP system 26 to the SMP. During the 
inspection, minor violations that can be corrected on-site are documented and followed up 
in the next inspections, while legal violations are referred for administrative action. 
Operation evaluation: Municipal Offices of Education conduct annual evaluations at 
individual schools to assess overall operations and management or the SMP. The evaluation 
covers key areas such as budget planning and execution, nutrition education and counselling, 
food quality control, and personnel management. 
Satisfaction survey: At least once a year, each school conducts a satisfaction survey targeting 
students and parents. The survey is preferably conducted in the first half of the year so that 
the feedback can be reflected in the operations in the second half. Survey results and 
corresponding improvement measures must be disclosed, for example, on the school website.

Lessons learned and best practices 
• 
School Meal Bulletin Board system: Under local ordinances, all schools must operate a 
School Meal Bulletin Board on their websites to promote transparency and facilitate 
communication with students and parents. The key functions are as follows: 
- Menu posting: Daily, weekly, and monthly menus, including ingredient origins, 
nutrition, allergens, and photos of the meals actually served to students. 
- Operational information: Disclosure of budgets, supplier contracts, and hygiene 
inspection results. 
- Educational content: Materials on food safety and healthy eating habits. 
- Feedback: Comments and suggestions submitted by students and parents. 
• 
Occupational health and safety in school kitchens27: In 2023, the Ministry of Education 
launched a national initiative to improve school kitchen environments, allocating funding 
to regional Offices of Education.

- Upgrading ventilation systems: Based on national guidelines, school kitchens are 
enhancing ventilation systems to expand exhaust capacity and fresh air intake, 
preventing health issues among cooking personnel caused by cooking fumes. 
- Reducing cooking emissions: To minimize cooking fumes, schools are replacing gas 
appliances with electric equipment and limit fried foods to no more than twice a 
week. 
- Separating work areas: Following the school meal HACCP system, schools are 
dividing kitchen spaces into separate zones for ingredient preparation, cooking, and 
dishwashing to improve working efficiency, food safety, and hygiene. 
• 
Recognition of Excellent Healthy Meal Schools 28: Since 2023, the Daegu Office of 
Education has selected Excellent Healthy Meal Schools annually. Nominated schools are 
evaluated by experts based on criteria such as nutrition standards, student satisfaction, 
and cost-effectiveness. Selected schools receive subsidies for special meals and staff 
incentives, and videos of award-winning menu preparation are shared on the National 
Education Information System (NEIS) to serve as references for other schools. 
 
Challenges   
• 
Reducing food waste: School food waste increased more than 2.5 times, from 13.55 
million kg in 2020 to 34.23 million kg in 2022.29 This was largely due to low student 
preference for nutritionally designed menus, such as vegetables, beans, and fish, and led 
to high leftovers, disposal costs, and environmental impact. Schools need to develop 
menus that balance nutrition with student preference, improve culinary skills of cooking 
staff, and provide education to raise awareness among students.  
• 
Protecting the health of cooking personnel30: School cooking personnel are exposed to 
health risks such as cooking fumes, which have been linked to lung cancer cases. The 
government has introduced lung cancer screening and established ventilation standards 
for school kitchens, but implementation remains uneven across regions due to limited 
funding and inconsistent policy attention. To ensure safer working conditions, expanded 
funding, stronger legal protections, and greater public awareness are necessary. 
• 
Promoting diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI)31: As of 2023, the annual growth rate of 
immigration in Korea reached 50.9%. Multicultural students accounted for 3.5% of all 
students, and this figure rose to 4.4% in elementary schools. The SMP should respond to 
this demographic shift by moving beyond the traditional emphasis on preserving Korean 
food culture and embracing the importance of inclusivity. To reflect diverse cultural and 
religious dietary needs, improvements may include offering multicultural menus, 
integrating food culture education into school programs, and providing alternative 
options for students with religious or medical restrictions. Enhancing facilities and 
expanding meal options for students with disabilities are also essential components of an 
inclusive SMP.

Acceptable range of the Nutritional Standards for School Meals 
 
 
For example, in the case of male students in grades 1 to 3 of elementary school, each school 
lunch should provide 570 kcal with an acceptable range of ±10% (i.e., between 513 and 627 
kcal). The energy composition should follow the recommended ratio of (carbohydrates: 
protein: fat) = (55-65% : 7-20% : 15-30%). At least 11.7g of protein should be provided, but 
protein should account for no more than 20% of the total energy intake (i.e., 513-627 kcal). 
For vitamin A, the recommended amount is 150 Retinol Activity Equivalents (R.A.E.) or more, 
with a minimum of 104 R.A.E. to meet the estimated average requirement.
