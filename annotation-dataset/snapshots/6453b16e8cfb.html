It may not have had the iconic imagery of Gemini’s Ed White floating in space, 
or the jaw-dropping chutzpah of Bruce McCandless’ untethered spacewalk, 
but video of Jared Isaacman, stepping into the void on the first commercial 
spacewalk is still set to become part of space history and is yet another sign of 
a bold new era in space. Critics might argue that the Polaris Dawn mission was 
only recreating what NASA and Soviet crews had achieved over 60 years ago 
and that such activity is now almost routine for government-funded astronauts 
living and working aboard the ISS. Yet this misses the point and the incredible 
trajectory of the company that has enabled this and that is blazing a trail for 
others to follow on this new frontier. 
It needs to be remembered that it is still only 14 years since SpaceX first 
launched and recovered Dragon, yet it now launches more satellites than 
governments. It is a highly adaptable capsule and trusted to deliver cargo 
and humans to the ISS. A development of Dragon will de-orbit the ISS, an 
orbital metaphor made real by the commercial sector literally shunting away 
government spaceflight in the race to create an LEO industry. Meanwhile, 
SpaceX is also poised to make its giant Starship operational – something that 
promises to slash the cost of getting mass to space and, if it delivers, and if 
the spacecraft is as flexible and adaptable as Dragon, then humanity’s future 
in space indeed looks bright. As we have already seen with reusable launchers 
and mega-constellations, where SpaceX leads, others will undoubtedly follow. 
Polaris Dawn’s brief crew excursion into the great expanse is, thus, another 
stepping stone on this path. Where will the next 14 years 
take us?

Wizz Air to stretch its legs from 
Gatwick with Airbus A321XLR 
AIR TRANSPORT
At a press conference in London on 10 September, Hungarian-
headquartered European ultra low-cost carrier, Wizz Air revealed that 
its first Airbus A321XLR services, beginning on 31 March 2025, will 
connect London Gatwick and Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. With fares to be 
priced from £135, CEO József Váradi said that this would open a new 
era in affordable medium-haul travel linking the UK and Saudi Arabia. 
The news followed an earlier announcement that will see 
A321XLR flights from Milan, Italy commence to Abu Dhabi, UAE 
from June 2025. Váradi says that, with 47 A321XLRs on order, the 
strategy will be to spread them around six to eight of its 33 operating 
bases. It currently operates a fleet of 233 Airbus narrowbodies. The 
airline will configure its A321XLRs in a 239-seat single-class layout 
with non-reclining seats. 
Set to be delivered to Wizz Air in February/March of 2025, the 
Airbus A321XLR, which received certification earlier this year, is the 
longest-ranged variant of the manufacturer's best-selling A321 single-
aisle with an extra fuel tank giving it a maximum range of 4,700nm. 
It also burns 30% less fuel than previous airliners in the same class. 
So far, Airbus has won over 500 orders for the A321XLR, including 
IndiGo, American Airlines, JetBlue and Qantas who see the jet's 
potential in connecting more 'long and thin' city pairs that could not 
support widebody services. 
Range anxiety? 
However, the A321XLR suffered a setback in the US recently when 
low-cost carrier, Frontier cancelled its order for the type and Spirit 
Airlines and JetBlue deferred their deliveries. Wizz Air chief Váradi 
downplayed concerns from Frontier about the actual range meeting its 
promised specification, saying that Wizz had been in contact with its 
fellow LCC about this, adding: “I don’t think range maximisation was 
ever our objective,” whereas Frontier had been specifically eyeing the 
transatlantic market.
He explained that Wizz Air would not be aiming to fly the A321XLR 
to the very limits of its endurance as turnaround times for its business 
model mean that a medium-haul flight and a short-haul flight allow for 
better efficiencies and maximum daily utilisation of the aircraft - rather 
than a single 8hr leg, which would allow for only one route. 
However, with the A321XLR extending the range of the current 
A321neo by two hours, Váradi  saw greater opportunities to link 
destinations in Europe with the Middle East, Africa and Central Asia. 
While the prime travel market of India was out of range from the UK, 
Váradi noted its potential from Wizz Air's bases in Eastern Europe, 
calling it “very exciting”. He admitted, though, that securing slots at 
Indian airports came with its own challenges.
Despite the range of the A321XLR, Wizz Air has ruled out entering 
the highly competitive transatlantic market – with Váradi pointing out that 
British Airways itself has 34 flights – allowing it to easily crush any new 
entrants with minimal effort, quipping: “We don't want to put [our airline] 
in the ocean” when refering to taking on the legacy airlines.
Gatwick looks to northern runway approval
Meanwhile, at the same press conference, Gatwick Airport CEO, Stewart 
Wingate revealed that its submission to increase capacity with its northern 
runway would be on the UK Aviation Minister's desk in November, with a final 
decision expected in early 2025. On the sidelines of the press conference, 
Wingate told AEROSPACE that he was heartened by the swift approval 
by the new government of London City's capacity increase, saying: “we are 
encouraged by what happened at London City and hope the government 
backs us.” The proposal, which Gatwick continued work on during the 
pandemic, would see the northern runway centreline shifted 39ft to the 
north to allow dual runway operations and allow capacity to increase to 75m 
passengers by the late 2030s. Wingate notes that construction for the £2.2bn 
plan, which requires no new land outside the current airport boundary, could 
begin as early as next year.
Summary
Jeddah, a coastal city on the Red Sea, and transit point for pilgrims to 
Mecca and Medina, is perhaps an unlikely first destination for a European 
ultra-low-cost carrier to connect to the UK, but it is emblematic of these new 
'long and thin' routes that the A321XLR is expected to open up. As a gateway 
to the fast-changing and youthful Saudi Arabia, this route is likely to pioneer 
increased air travel and tourism to the Kingdom, ahead of major airline pushes 
by flag carrier, Saudia and new start-up Riyadh Air aiming to realise the 
country's ambitious Vision 2030 plan. Moreover, with tickets to start at £135, 
this may show that the era of low-fare travel is not yet over as budget airlines 
use the latest fuel-sipping aircraft available to extend the low-cost model 
further and further afield.

With the advantage of having time on their hands, an RAF C-17 team was able to thoroughly 
evaluate the risk associated with an emergency mission and successfully evacuate almost 
200 people from South Sudan. AM SEAN REYNOLDS CB CBE DFC discusses the risk 
mitigation lessons from this flight.
May the holes in your 
Swiss cheese never 
line up
“M
ay the holes in your Swiss 
cheese never line up.” Those 
were the words expressed 
very eloquently by a senior 
test pilot during his leaving speech from the RAF 
after more than 30 years of distinguished service. 
This caused a knowing chuckle from the audience of 
aviator friends that had assembled to say ‘thank you’ 
and ‘farewell’. But what did he mean? Clearly this was 
not some advice to be taken and followed up on our 
next visit to the deli counter in Sainsbury’s.
Swiss cheese
I am guessing that many of those reading this will 
have come across Professor James Reason’s model 
of the Swiss cheese of accident causation, which is 
used in risk analysis and risk management with the 
principle being layered defences. 
The model likens human systems to multiple 
slices of Swiss cheese, which have randomly placed 
and sized holes in each slice, stacked side by side. 
The risk of a threat becoming a reality is mitigated by 
the differing layers and types of defences which are 
‘stacked’ behind each other. 
Therefore, in theory, lapses and weaknesses in 
one defence do not allow a risk to materialise (eg 
a hole in each slice in the stack aligning with holes 
in all the other slices of cheese) to prevent a single 
point of failure. 
Layered defences
Much of what we do in aviation, and other safety- 
critical activities, has these layered defences built 
into it. As technology has evolved the way we do 
business, and increased efficiency and safety, it 
has also increased complexity of the processes.

the knowledge and competence of the individual 
to safe systems of work. The use of Standard

prime examples of this. However, the human is still 
at the centre of these processes and is often the 
last line of defence that can block the holes in the 
cheese, something that I was to find out first hand 
just before Christmas in 2013.
At the time, I was in command of the RAF’s No

lessons that had been learnt from the loss of a

Evacuation mission
On 18 December 2013, most of the world seemed 
to be enjoying the run-up to Christmas. However, 
despite a relentless demand for support to operations

deteriorating security situation in South Sudan and

the South Sudanese capital, Juba, with a heavy crew

had a team from the Foreign & Commonwealth 
Office (FCO) to process the EPs and ensure that we 
were only evacuating those who were entitled. 
During the planning process we had tried to 
identify most of the operational risks and then put 
mitigations in place. We even formed a ‘Red Team’ 
to try and pick apart the plan and identify the known 
risks, but we knew that the main tactical risk – which 
was the deteriorating security situation around Juba

by the fact that we would arrive at first light. 
A need to know
This is known as operational security (OPSEC) with 
only those who really needed to know being aware. 
Other risk mitigations were the heavy crew to manage

crew were qualified to fly on night vision goggles

were all specially selected for this flight in terms of

party for the hour that the aircraft would be on 
the ground and a team from the British Embassy, 
including the Defence Attaché (DA), who would be at 
the airport to smooth any local difficulties that might 
arise. Finally, the weather for the next 48 hours in that 
part of Central Africa looked good.

to turn the aircraft around. However, after some

Accident investigation reporting
The early reporting of aircraft accidents is vital. NICHOLAS J BUTCHER FRAeS and 
Dr JOHN BARNETT CEng FCLIT consider the effectiveness of ICAO Annex 13 and how 
the publication of such timely accident reports could still be avoided.
Deafening silence
I
n the January 2023 edition of AEROSPACE, 
the RAeS Flight Operations Group identified 
the importance of the early reporting of 
aircraft accidents, in particular the need for 
the timely publication of accident investigation 
reports. Without such final reports being published, 
safety recommendations may not be adopted and 
important lessons might not be learnt.
The International Civil Aviation Organization 
(ICAO) derives its constitution and remit for the 
safety of international aviation from the Chicago 
Convention of 1944. ICAO Annex 13 Aircraft 
Accident and Incident Investigation specifies 
the procedures for the investigation of aircraft 
accidents, as well as the criteria for publication of 
accident reports. Annex 13 states that, if a final 
report cannot be made available within 12 months 
of the accident, the Contracting State responsible 
for conducting the investigation shall make an 
interim statement publicly available on each 
anniversary of the occurrence, detailing the progress 
of the investigation and any safety issues identified.
Justifiable delays
Most accident investigations will involve a wide 
range of factors requiring lengthy study, sometimes 
including time-consuming research. Thus, delays in 
publication of a final accident report can sometimes 
be justifiable. In most aircraft accident investigations 
the ICAO Annex 13 criteria works very well. Interim 
and final accident reports on major accidents and 
incidents are usually published without delay. Then 
associated findings and recommendations are 
normally accepted and implemented by relevant 
interested parties. 
However, some recent aircraft accidents have 
not resulted in accident reports being issued in 
a timely matter without good reason and there 
have been several occasions when interim or 
final accident reports have never been issued. 
The passage of several years without either an 
interim or a final accident report being published is 
unacceptable and clearly not in the best interest of 
worldwide aviation safety.
In the past some ICAO Contracting States have 
claimed that there were possible terrorist activities 
involved in accidents and, therefore, it was not 
in their best security interests to issue a final 
accident report. 
Aircraft accident investigations and final 
accident reports should, as a minimum, identify 
the cause of an accident and result in appropriate 
safety recommendations being made.

IATA’s position
In a June 2023 press release(1), the International 
Air Transport Association (IATA) identified that, in 
the period from 2018 to 2022, only 96 of 214 
aircraft accident investigations conformed to the 
requirements of the Chicago Convention. Also, 
over the past five years, fewer than 50% of the 
required aircraft accident reports met the standards 
for being thorough and being published on time. In 
cases where identified errors and accident causes, 
together with safety recommendations and their 
implementations, have not been shared with the 
international aviation community, then similar and 
wholly preventable accidents might occur in future. 
IATA’s 2023 Annual Safety Report stated 
that less than 49% of accidents in commercial 
scheduled passenger and cargo category between 
January 2018 and December 2023 were not 
subject to the publication of a final accident report.
IATA presented a working paper at the ICAO 
14th Air Navigation Conference, addressing these 
important shortfalls in accident reporting. IATA is 
working with States through the ICAO Accident 
Investigation Panel to understand why accident 
reports are not published in accordance with ICAO 
Annex 13. To support this initiative a five point 
roadmap has been developed by IATA. 
The IATA working paper made the following 
conclusion: “The accident investigation process 
is one of the most important learning tools when 
building global safety standards. But to learn from 
an accident, the aviation industry needs reports that 
are complete, accessible and timely. States should 
follow the requirements laid out in ICAO Annex 
13 and publish thorough final reports of accident 
investigations in accordance with the timelines 
required by this Annex, to guarantee the continuous 
improvements of aviation safety standards.” 
This IATA working paper was jointly presented 
by the International Business Aviation Council 
(IBAC) and the International Federation of Air Line 
Pilots’ Associations (IFALPA).
Potential conflicts of interests
ICAO Annex 13 (Chapter 3.2) specifies that “A 
State shall establish an accident investigation 
authority that is independent from State aviation 
authorities and other entities that could interfere 
with the conduct or objectivity of an investigation.”
In the US, the National Transportation Safety 
Board (NTSB) reports directly to the US Congress. 
It is totally independent from the Federal Aviation 
Administration (FAA) and the US Department of 
Transportation (DoT). Similarly, in the UK, the Air 
Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB) reports 
directly to the Department for Transport (DfT), and 
is totally separate from the CAA. 
While aircraft investigation agencies of 
most ICAO Contracting States have clear 
demarcation lines for responsibility of all aspects 
of an investigation, perhaps for some States 
the independence of the investigation agency 
might not always be as clear-cut. It could be the 
case that some organisations responsible for the 
oversight of flight safety, both flight operations 
and airworthiness, are also responsible for the 
investigation of aircraft accidents. 
In such circumstances this has the potential 
to create a conflict of interest. The governments 
of some ICAO Contracting States may own or 
support their ‘national’ carrier. If an accident were 
to occur which identified serious shortcomings 
by the operator, its personnel or its management 
structure, then it might be an embarrassment to 
their country to publish a critical report. 
However, the failure of an ICAO Contracting 
State to publish accident information based on 
thorough investigation in accordance with ICAO 
Annex 13, should be considered an abrogation of 
their national and international responsibilities.

Taking a ‘giant leap’ for commercial spaceflight, the crew of the Polaris Dawn mission 
conducted the world’s first private ‘spacewalk’ in September. KATE ARKLESS-GRAY reports.
A new 
dawn in 
space
P
olaris Dawn, which blasted off from  
Complex 39A of NASA’s Kennedy Space 
Center at 10.23BST on 10 September 
was the second spaceflight for billionaire, 
Jared ‘Rook’ Isaacman, who flew on the first all-
civilian orbital spaceflight Inspiration4 in 2021. It 
is the first of three planned missions in the Polaris 
Programme. Joining him in the SpaceX Dragon 
capsule, Resilience (coincidentally the capsule used 
on Inspriation4) were pilot, Scott ‘Kidd’ Poteet, and 
mission specialists, Anna Menon and Sarah Gillis, 
who became the first SpaceX employees to fly to 
space on one of their vehicles. 
Ahead of the spacewalk, the capsule was put in 
an elliptical Earth orbit, taking the crew further into 
space than any humans since Apollo 17 in 1972. 
With a maximum altitude of 870 miles, Menon and 
Gillis have now flown further from Earth than any 
women in history. In preparation for the spacewalk, 
the orbit was lowered to 458 miles, and the crew 
prepared themselves for the ultimate suit test. 
Unlike the International Space Station (ISS), 
Dragon has no airlock, so the entire capsule had 
to be depressurised before the hatch could be 
opened. This also meant that all four crew members 
were relying on the new SpaceX spacesuits to 
keep them alive. The suits were pressurised and a 
series of leak checks were conducted before the 
final depressurisation of the capsule and, once the 
hatch was opened, a new record was set by the four 
astronauts forming the largest number of people 
exposed to the vacuum of space at any one time.  
 
Skywalker
The spacewalk began at 11.12BST on 12 September 
and, during the extravehicular activity (EVA), Isaacman 
and Gillis took turns to partially leave the spacecraft, 
each spending approximately eight minutes testing 
the suit for movability and comfort. A big issue with 
spacesuits is that they can be bulky and difficult to 
manoeuvre, making spacewalks from the ISS – which 
usually last around 6.5 hours – a test of physical, as 
well as mental, stamina. 
Widely reported as a full spacewalk, the Polaris 
Dawn EVA was technically a Stand-up EVA (SEVA), 
since the astronauts did not fully leave the capsule. 
Their feet remained inside and connected to footholds 
on the ‘Skywalker’ equipment that was fitted to Dragon 
for this mission.

 The very first spacewalk was back in 1965, when 
Soviet cosmonaut, Alexei Leonov stepped out of the 
fabric airlock attached to his Voskhod 2 spacecraft

had been a resounding success, the truth of this 
daring feat eventually emerged. With the pressure

it ballooned, leaving him floating within it. Upon 
returning to the airlock, he struggled to get back in,

having “all the flexibility of a rusty suit of armour.”

The new SpaceX suits look very sleek and

a former test engineer who worked on the MMU.

a mini-spacecraft in itself, capable of supporting a 
six-hour EVA, without an umbilical.” Stein raises an

mission has pushed the envelope of what private 
companies are able to achieve, this suit is a long way 
from being a replacement for the ISS EVA suits. 
While some people are optimistic that the

there is a lot to be done before they could support 
such activity. For any spacewalk lasting more than a 
couple of hours, suits will require an active thermal 
control system and, to be truly useful, life-support 
systems need to be part of the spacesuit, to remove 
the limitation of a permanent tether to the spacecraft. 
That said, this test is an exciting step towards future 
spacesuits and it is good to see that they are testing 
them with men and women, rather than relying on the 
‘one-size-fits-all’ approach of the past.

people across the globe together. 
Whether you class it as a stand-up EVA or a full 
spacewalk, Polaris Dawn’s mission was inherently 
risky, and the crew successfully rose to the challenge 
of testing a commercial spacesuit, in space. In doing 
so, they no doubt inspired thousands of people on the 
ground, and who knows, one of them could grow up 
to be the first person to step on Mars.

Sustainable Aviation Fuel
Is Sustainable Aviation Fuel the ‘silver bullet’ that some claim it is in 
reducing aviation’s climate impact? Ahead of the UK SAF mandate to be 
introduced in 2025, RAeS Head of Research, NAOMI ALLEN MRAeS 
assesses its potential.
100% SAF is 
not 100% of 
the solution
I
n August, the UK’s Advertising Standards 
Agency (ASA) upheld complaints that a radio 
advertisement about Virgin Atlantic’s Flight 
100 gave a misleading impression of the 
environmental impact of using 100% Sustainable 
Aviation Fuel (SAF). 
The ASA ruled that a significant proportion of 
listeners would interpret ‘100% SAF’ as meaning 
‘100% sustainable’. While a transatlantic flight 
using only SAF is an admirable achievement, 
and represents a useful step forwards in 
understanding the technological and operational 
implications of SAF for aviation, the ASA’s ruling 
is a reminder that we should be realistic about 
the achievable benefits, and avoid setting public 
expectations too high. 
Maintaining credibility
It is important to maintain credibility and ensure 
that the industry is transparent in terms of the 
emissions benefits offered by SAF. However, 
what those benefits actually are is a more difficult 
question to answer than might first appear.

For Flight 100, the project team had calculated 
a 64% reduction in CO2e compared with an 
equivalent flight using petroleum-based jet fuel 
(although it is worth noting that they attribute 
some of this to operational efficiencies that were 
implemented for the flight and, therefore, the 
SAF contribution to the emissions reduction is 
presumably somewhat lower than this). 
However, SAF is not made equal in terms of 
environmental impact and its provenance has 
a significant impact on its green credentials. 
Whether the level of emissions reduction seen 
during this test flight can be extrapolated as we 
scale up SAF and use it on more flights, will be 
crucial to fully understanding the potential long-
term benefits. Looking at ICAO’s documentation 
on the Life Cycle Emissions of CORSIA 
(Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for 
International Aviation) eligible fuels yields an array 
of numbers for different variants of SAF. These 
encompass both different feedstocks and different 
production processes, all with their own emissions 
implications. Taking into account land-use effects, 
the spread of emissions factors is huge, including 
some SAF types with a Life Cycle Emissions 
Factor higher than that of petroleum-based jet fuel. 
However, other variants of SAF are highly effective 
in reducing emissions over the full life cycle. 
SAF mandate
It is notable that the UK SAF mandate, which will 
come into effect at the start of 2025, introduces a 
cap on SAF produced through the Hydroprocessed 
Esters and Fatty Acids (HEFA) process that 
comes in over the period of the mandate. There 
is also a requirement for gradually increasing 
quantities of SAF produced through the Power-to-
Liquid process (which uses energy to create the 
fuel from carbon dioxide and hydrogen, and can, 
therefore, be highly efficient at reducing emissions 
depending on the source of the resources used). 
This generally represents a move from SAF 
variants which can be available earlier, but are less 
‘clean’ to alternatives which offer a greater level of 
emissions reduction, but will take longer to scale 
up. However, will it be enough to take us to net 
zero by 2050? Estimates of how much SAF can 
reduce carbon emissions vary, but are often quoted 
as up to 70-80% but it is unclear when this level 
of emissions reduction is likely to be reached, as it 
will be dependent on the rate of production scale-
up for different variants of SAF. 
Magnitude of the problem
Another aspect to consider is whether we 
are being clear about the magnitude of the 
problem we are trying to solve in our journey to 
decarbonisation. Many of the decarbonisation 
pathways published by the aviation industry only 
consider ‘tank-to-wake’ emissions, omitting any 
consideration of the emissions from creating 
the fuel. This enables them to assume 100% 
emissions reduction through the use of SAF. 
ATAG’s Waypoint 2050 scenarios are one 
example of this. The result of this is that the 
residual emissions after accounting for overall 
reductions through technology, operations and 
fuels are underestimated compared with taking the 
full ‘well-to-wake’ emissions into account. 
It is, therefore, encouraging that some recent 
pathways (for example the International Council 
on Clean Transportation’s Vision 2050) are taking 
the full life cycle emissions of the fuel into account, 
allowing a more complete forecast of the residual 
emissions at 2050. 
In moving to scale up SAF production – and 
increase the proportion of it used in the fleet 
– it is, therefore, essential that this is done in 
a considered manner, and prioritising options 
which offer the greatest opportunity for emissions 
reduction at an affordable price. Power-to-Liquid 
SAF will represent one of the most efficient means 
of reducing emissions through fuel, provided that 
the energy used in its production and transport 
comes from renewable sources. However, that 
in itself raises questions – aviation will not be 
the only industry requiring significant amounts of 
green energy to decarbonise, and it is by no means 
certain that supply will meet demand. 
It is clear that regardless of how quickly 
we can scale up SAF, and the proportion of 
Power-to-Liquid SAF available, there will still be 
emissions from the production of the fuel. SAF 
cannot, therefore, be the sole means by which 
to decarbonise aviation and the industry must 
continue complementary actions. Reducing the 
amount of fuel required will always have a positive 
environmental impact, be it conventional fuel, 
SAF or alternative fuels, such as hydrogen. It is, 
therefore, imperative to improve the efficiency 
of the fleet, both through replacing older aircraft 
with current state-of-the-art technology, and 
through the development of a new generation 
of more efficient aircraft and those using 
novel technologies. It is also increasingly being 
recognised across the sector that, in addition to 
improvements in technology, operations and fuels, 
residual emissions will require the use of carbon 
removals. 
While SAF will have a key role to play in 
decarbonising aviation, and Flight 100 was an 
important step along that road, it is clear that there 
is still much work to be done in understanding 
the complete solution needed to reach net zero. 
Transparency in the expected benefits from the 
multiple technologies and activities which will be 
required can only be an advantage in ensuring 
realistic public expectations.

On 9 July 2024, the European space industry 
reached an historic milestone with the successful 
launch of the Ariane 6, thrilling space enthusiasts 
worldwide. Among the 11 notable payloads on its 
upper stage was YPSat, a project driven by over 
100 dedicated young professionals. This inspiring 
initiative began in 2021 with a visionary goal of 
empowering young professionals to respond to 
Arianespace’s call for proposals, allowing them to 
work on a hands-on project, alongside their primary 
job at the European Space Agency (ESA).
The YPSat team set out with a clear objective: 
to develop a payload capable of capturing videos 
of the fairing and the deployment of other ESA 
CubeSats, as well as hosting a scientific smaller 
payload, the OSCAR-QUBE. The challenge was 
immense but, with the support of more than 80 
experienced experts, the testing phase yielded 
promising results. 
The maiden flight of Ariane 6 was a particularly 
significant moment for all YPSat team members. 
The names of the entire team were engraved on 
the structure, symbolising their contribution, and the 
team eagerly anticipated receiving data during the 
two-minute signal window. The mission’s immense 
success delighted everyone at ESA, with only 
nine (0.01%) of the space packets experiencing 
corruption—seven due to desynchronisation and 
two due to transmission errors. Everything worked 
perfectly, despite the CubeSat ejection occurring 
eight seconds later than planned.
Indeed, the team successfully retrieved 30 
seconds of HD video during the fairing separation 
and 15 seconds of HD video capturing the 
deployment of two ESA Education CubeSats 
(ISTSat-1 and 3Cat-4) from the ExoLaunch 
dispenser. Additionally, six pictures of space and 
Earth were retrieved. For the scientific objectives, 
YPSat is awaiting data from the OSCAR-QUBE 
mission analysis to obtain information along the 
payload’s trajectory. The mission analysis team is 
also still working to provide reference data along the 
trajectory, to compare it to simulations and analyse 
the preliminary versus flight trajectory.
A documentary is available that offers an 
overview of the incredible team working on this 
project. YPSat is the epitome of ESA’s fantastic 
missions, showcasing the ability to develop a fully 
operational payload from design to launch in less 
than three years. This tremendous success paves 
the way for other young professional initiatives, such 
as YPSat-2 that is being created.

V-BOMBERS
Britain’s Nuclear Frontline in 
the Cold War
Dr Tony Redding
Grub Street, 2024, 360pp, £25
When the last Vulcan in RAF service (display 
purposes only) retired, the UK had 31 combat air 
squadrons and, in the words of former Defence 
Select Committee Chair Tobias Ellwood, “now we 
may struggle to have seven”.  At a time when a 
German commentator recently noted that “Europe’s 
bonsai military forces have nurtured bonsai 
industries,” it is salutary to recall that, when I first 
flew solo, RAF Bomber Command consisted of 104 
nuclear-armed Victors and Vulcans (13 squadrons) 
plus 72 Canberras and 24 Valiants assigned to 
SACEUR. Furthermore, the British aviation industry 
could produce three (four if you include the Sperrin) 
separate V-bombers pretty much to time, cost and 
on spec. 
Dr Tony Redding has written what he describes 
as ‘the story of the very British deterrent’. He has 
written a mighty tome of 360 pages. He accepts 
that much has been written about the V-bombers, 
‘but virtually nothing has been said about their 
strategic nuclear role’, which is surprising, given that 
his bibliography contains books and articles which 
do just that. To cut to the chase, Dr Redding has 
amassed a host of reminiscences/war stories from 
largely Vulcan ‘old and bold’ (Valiant and Victor folks 
get barely a mention), and if you want to read well-
illustrated yarns from the ‘flatiron’ force, this is the 
book for you. However, it is quite clear that the author 
has no front-line experience and is not an aviator, 
and it shows in his lack of critical evaluation of what 
he has been told. For example, a Vulcan captain, who 
never had to operate with US Project E weapons 
(Valiants and Canberras), is seriously quoted as 
saying that if the balloon went up, “the first job of 
the RAF duty officer was to shoot the American 
[custodian] and grab the key.” Fantasy world. Then 
again, an operational analysis bean counter is quoted 
as saying that ”if you joined the RAF hoping to fly a 
Lightning and ended up on QRA, it must have been 
a bit frustrating.” Makes you wonder what the 5/11 
Sqn QRA sheds at Binbrook were for?
The chapters are unstructured, and they would 
benefit from serious editing by someone who 
understands aviation. For example, the Vulcan 
limiting Mach number is given as both 0.975 and 
0.93, but the Vulcan B1A never got a mention, nor 
did the Victor B1, which once went supersonic.  
What of potential strategic war fighting on which 
the author aims to focus? Here we run up against 
that bane of all historians: tricky memories. When 
looking back at the Cuban Missile Crisis, the former 
CO of 100 Sqn is quoted that, on 27 October 1963, 
“all available Victor aircraft and crews at Wittering 
were brought to cockpit readiness. Each aircraft was 
loaded with a free-fall thermonuclear weapon…”  
Except that half the Wittering Wing - 139 Sqn - was 
converting to Blue Steel and 100 Sqn, which had 
only reformed five months earlier and was heavily in 
the throes of standing up. Prime Minister Macmillan 
had made it clear to the Chief of Air Staff that he did 
not wish Bomber Command to be alerted and ‘that 
matters should be played as low key as possible.’ 
There is no mention in the 3 Group Operational 
Record Book (ORB) of any increase in the number 
of Victors on QRA. As for the 100 Sqn ORB, there 
is only a passing mention of Cuba in the October 
entry and none the following month. The Squadron 
sports programme continued uninterrupted with 
rugby, soccer and hockey fixtures on 31 October. All 
in all, there was no sign of any 100 Sqn heightened 
state of readiness, which shows that the memory 
can play tricks over the mists of time.      
In summary, V-Bombers is a curate’s egg. 
There are evocative photos of former crews 
on detachments and hosts of stirring yarns 
and reminiscences from a bygone age. But 
serious history, this is not.  It is predominantly an 
unstructured ramble with no firm grip on what it 
was like at the time. The book cries out for focusing 
because it contains little that is new. Given the 
relative lack of Victor and Valiant perspectives, this 
book should more properly be titled VULCAN-Force, 
only recommended for those who enjoy a stroll 
down memory lane.     
Wg Cdr Andrew Brookes FRAeS

PROPELLER TWILIGHT
The Last Generation of British 
Piston Engine Fighters
by Tony Buttler FRAeS
Crecy, 2023, 224pp, £34.95. (Copy in the National 
Aerospace Library)
On 9 August 1952, a Royal Navy Hawker Sea Fury 
shot down a North Korean MiG-15 jet fighter. This 
event was, in effect, the swansong of the generation 
of fast, propeller-driven fighter aircraft that had 
dominated the skies during and immediately 
following WW2.
There have been many books written about the 
squadron service and achievements of the various 
propeller-driven fighter aircraft flown by the RN and 
the RAF in the 1950s, but this fascinating book by 
Tony Buttler takes a completely different approach 
by discussing the development and flight testing of 
the last generation of British piston engine fighter 
aircraft which were used in the later stages of WW2 
and in the years immediately following the war.
The author, who is well known in the Society’s 
circles and is a member of the Aeronautical 
Heritage Group Committee, starts by considering 
the engines that powered these aircraft. 
The immensely complicated in-line engines, 
manufactured by Napier (Sabre) and Rolls-Royce 
(Merlin, Griffon and Eagle) were liquid cooled and 
were able to present a much lower frontal area 
than the mechanically simpler, Centaurus air-cooled 
rotary engines, manufactured by Bristol.
He then moves on to look at the development 
of the various propellers which were manufactured 
by de Havilland and Rotol. As engine power output 
increased, attention turned to the development 
of contra-rotating propellers to present increased 
blade area to absorb the extra power.
Having discussed the various power plant 
issues, the author then moves on to write in great 
detail on the many famous fighter aircraft of the 
era, including the Supermarine Spitfire and Seafire, 
the Hawker Tempest, Fury and Sea Fury, and the 
de Havilland Hornet and Sea Hornet. Finally, in 
the appendices, the author describes some of the 
overseas projects which used UK engines.
Each chapter contains a great deal of technical 
detail, together with numerous black and white 
vintage photographs of the aircraft concerned. 
The photographs, taken both in the air and on the 
ground, illustrate the various development aspects 
that were incorporated into the final versions of 
the aircraft. There are also many quotes from 
test reports prepared by the RAF Central Fighter 
Establishment.
Altogether, this book contains a wealth of 
data about the final versions of the generation of 
propeller-driven fighter aircraft developed in the UK 
and must be the starting point for any student of 
that era.

A fully British system
One £22m MoD-owned 150kg satellite, built by 
Surrey Satellite Technology (an Airbus Defence 
and Space subsidiary), does not yet provide full 
coverage but the aim is to install a constellation of 
small satellites into orbit – a major UK technological 
capability supporting military and civil operations. 
The latter includes climate change and natural 
disaster monitoring, and the full system will include a 
range of sensors allowing all-weather observations, 
as well as some in-orbit flexibility. 
However, it is the system’s autonomy that 
counts – often referred to as a ‘sovereign 
capability’. Tyche is best described as a technology 
demonstrator with an expected five-year life in low-
Earth orbit, with the complete constellation set to be 
fully operational by 2031. 
The National Audit Office recently criticised the 
government of the day for the slow and ineffective 
implementation of the 2021 National Space 
Strategy. Attention has focused on attempts to 
create ‘spaceports’ to launch rockets and expand 
commitments to human spaceflight, which in my 
mind, are questionable investments, yet Tyche plays 
to an established UK strength and will support a 
growth space industry.
