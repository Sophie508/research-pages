Time to lift the international  
sanctions on Syria? 
SUMMARY 
Since the unexpected overthrow of Bashar al-Assad's regime in early December 2024, Syria has 
embarked on an uncertain trajectory. Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), the armed jihadi group leading 
the offensive against the Assad forces, has now taken charge of the country and set up a caretaker 
government. Scepticism abounds in international circles about HTS owing to the group's terrorist 
credentials and Salafist ideology. However, the new Syrian authorities have declared plans to 
establish a political transition inclusive of all minorities and segments of Syrian society, as well as 
increased engagement with neighbouring countries and other foreign players, offering the 
international community some reassurance. 
One of the main demands in the current context from all sides, within Syria as well as from other 
states and organisations, has been to lift the complex web of international economic, financial and 
trade sanctions against the country. Most of these sanctions were imposed after Assad's brutal 
crackdown on protesters in 2011. Moreover, calls have been made to remove the designations of 
HTS and its leader Ahmed al-Sharaa from the international terrorist lists. Such steps are believed to 
be essential in addressing the significant economic and humanitarian challenges facing the country 
after nearly 14 years of civil war. 
The United States (US) imposes the most comprehensive sanctions on Syria, including secondary 
sanctions on foreign governments, non-US individuals and entities doing business with the Syrian 
government and sanctioned entities in Syria. The European Union (EU) has also imposed restrictive 
measures on certain Syrian economic sectors, along with asset freezes and travel bans on individuals 
or entities supporting the Assad regime. In January 2025, the US granted short-term waivers relating 
to the provision of basic services in response to requests for sanctions relief for Syria. Similarly, the 
EU Member States reached a political agreement to suspend certain restrictions gradually and 
conditionally. The UN Security Council has the authority to remove the terrorist designations of HTS 
and its leader from the ISIL (Da'esh)/Al-Qaida list.

Upheaval in Syria: The emerging order post-Assad 
The lightning offensive launched in late November 2024 by armed rebel groups spearheaded by 
Hayat Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), a jihadi group ruling Idlib in northwest Syria since 2017, led to the 
toppling of Syria's six decade-long Baathist regime in less than two weeks. As opposition forces took 
control of Damascus, Syria's dictator, Bashar al-Assad, fled to Moscow on 8 December 2024, where 
he was granted asylum. HTS leader Ahmed al-Sharaa (also known as Abu Mohammad al-Jolani) 
became Syria's de facto ruler. Mohammad al-Bashir, the former leader of Idlib's 'National Salvation 
Government', was placed at the helm of the caretaker government, to govern until 1 March 2025. 
Internally, the HTS leadership has taken several measures to ensure continuity of governance and 
prevent anarchy in Syria, for instance, by working with former regime officials to preserve state 
institutions and making efforts to disarm and integrate the various armed groups into the security 
forces. HTS has also gone to some lengths to assure the international community that it intends to 
be inclusive towards Syria's Alawite, Christian, Druze, Kurdish and other minorities. Regarding the 
political transition, Ahmed al-Sharaa stated in early January 2025 that drafting a new constitution 
and organising elections could take up to four years, the time necessary to stabilise the economy 
and organise a national dialogue among Syria's various political, ethnic and sectarian factions. The 
country remains fragmented since the ousting of the Assad regime, marked by continued fighting 
between the US-backed Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF) and the Turkey-backed Syrian 
National Army (SNA) in northeast Syria. Furthermore, there is continued interference from external 
players,1 latent unrest in the Alawite-majority areas, and efforts to subvert the new administration. 
Concerns are also mounting about HTS's purported governance plans for Syria (which reportedly 
include a centralised presidential system based on Islamic sharia law) and its increasing influence 
over the state apparatus. On 29 January 2024, Ahmed al-Sharaa was declared president for a 
transitional phase and empowered to form a temporary legislative council, while the constitution 
was suspended. The dissolution of all armed groups involved in the ousting of al-Assad, as well as 
the former government's army and security agencies and the Ba'ath party, was also announced. 
Externally, the HTS-led caretaker government has been portraying itself as the legitimate 
representative of the Syrian state and has increased its engagement with foreign players. During 
their first few weeks in power, the new authorities have welcomed delegations from Jordan, Türkiye, 
Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the United Nations, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, the US, and the EU. 
There is a broad consensus in Syria regarding the need to lift international sanctions imposed on 
the country and HTS itself. HTS and its leader, al-Sharaa, are on the UN Security Council counter-
terrorist sanctions list. Several countries and organisations, including the US and the EU, have 
imposed unilateral sanctions on Syria. Calls to ease the sanctions have been made by UN officials, 
to address the dire economic and humanitarian situation in Syria and encourage foreign investment 
and reconstruction efforts. The US has eased some restrictions relating to the provision of basic 
services, while EU foreign ministers reached a political agreement on 27 January 2025 to suspend 
some EU sanctions. For Western states, sanctions relief remains contingent on the new Syrian 
authorities' commitment to deliver an inclusive political transition. The UN special envoy to Syria 
argues that 'a credible process leading to a new transitional government involving all strands of 
Syrian society' would be the best way to secure the lifting of international sanctions against Syria. 
Overview of international sanctions on Syria 
Syria is one of the world's most sanctioned countries. The US designated it as a state sponsor of 
terrorism in 1979 and has been imposing sanctions on it ever since. Most other international 
sanctions against Syria date since 2011, following the Assad regime's violent suppression of protests.

United Nations Security Council (UNSC) sanctions 
The UNSC has not imposed any economic sanctions against Syria. In fact, its actions towards Syria 
since 2011 have been marked by division. Permanent UNSC members China and Russia have 
repeatedly vetoed resolutions mandating stronger measures against the Assad regime, including 
economic sanctions. However, the UNSC has adopted some resolutions calling for an end to the 
violence, authorising the delivery of humanitarian aid and condemning the use of chemical weapons 
in Syria. In particular, UNSC resolution 2254 (2015) of December 2015 set out a roadmap for a peace 
process in Syria, based on a 'Syrian-led, Syrian-owned political transition to end the conflict', and 
reiterated the need to suppress terrorism. 
Two UNSC sanctions regimes relate to Syria: 
• 
Sanctions in response to the terrorist bombing in Beirut, Lebanon, 14 February 2005. 
The UN Security Council Committee established pursuant to Resolution 1636 (2005) has 
listed individuals suspected of being involved in the bombing that resulted in the deaths 
of former Lebanese Prime Minister Rafiq Hariri and 22 others. The regime applies to both 
Syria and Lebanon. Currently, no individuals figure on the sanctions list.2 
• 
Sanctions against the Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (Da'esh) and Al-Qaida. 
The UNSC sanctions most relevant to the situation in Syria involve the counter-terrorist 
designations of HTS and its leader under UNSC Resolutions 1267 (1999) 1989 (2011) and 
2253 (2015) concerning the Islamic State in Iraq and the Levant (Da'esh), Al-Qaida and 
associated individuals, groups, undertakings and entities. The 1267 UNSC Committee 
listed Abu Mohammad al-Jolani, on 24 July 2013, as the leader of the Al-Nusrah Front 
for the People of the Levant, a jihadi group operating in Syria and affiliated with Al-
Qaida. The Al-Nusrah Front is listed since 2014. In 2016, al-Jolani announced that the Al-
Nusrah Front had changed its name to Jabhat Fath al-Sham and was no longer affiliated 
with any external entity. However, evidence indicated that the group remained aligned 
with Al-Qaida and continued to engage in terrorist activities. In January 2017, the Al-
Nusrah Front formed Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham. In June 2018, the Sanctions Committee 
updated the listing for the Al-Nusrah Front to include HTS as one of its aliases. 
To preserve Syria's cultural heritage, UNSC Resolution 2199 (2015) also prohibits any trade in 
antiquities removed from Syria since 15 March 2011. All states must institute an arms embargo, asset 
freeze and travel ban against persons and entities designated under the UNSC sanctions regimes. 
European Union restrictive measures 
The EU has several sanctions regimes in place regarding Syria. These include a regime imposing 
economic, financial and trade restrictions relating to the violent repression of the civilian population, 
a EU global human rights sanctions regime; a non-proliferation of chemical weapons regime; 
restrictive measures with respect to ISIL (Da'esh) and Al-Qaida, and restrictive measures in relation 
to the 14 February 2005 terrorist bombing in Beirut, Lebanon. The latter two are UNSC regimes that 
the EU is obliged to enforce. Additionally, the EU sanctions several Syrian individuals and entities 
for their mercenary activities in Ukraine. 
Restrictive measures in view of the situation in Syria 
On 9 May 2011, in response to the crackdown on anti-regime protests in Syria inspired by the Arab 
Spring uprisings across the region, the EU imposed autonomous restrictive measures on Syria and 
the persons responsible for the violent repression. Additionally, it suspended all bilateral 
cooperation with the Syrian government. The European Investment Bank (EIB) ceased to provide 
loans and technical assistance to Syria. Initially, the restrictive measures imposed by Council 
Decision 2011/273/CFSP and Council Regulation (EU) No 442/2011 included an embargo on arms 
and dual-use items, along with an asset freeze and travel bans on designated individuals and entities. 
On 23 May 2011, the Council added President al-Assad and several of his family members to the

318 individuals and 86 entities were listed under this sanctions regime, including several

The EU sanctions regime on Syria does not prohibit the export of food, medicines or medical equipment to 
Syria and does not target Syria's healthcare system. It also includes a wide-ranging humanitarian exception 
for the provision of humanitarian assistance to any part of the country. The EU has introduced further 
humanitarian exemptions and derogations, such as exemptions to facilitate the speedy delivery of 
humanitarian aid after the earthquake of 6 February 2023 (extended until 1 June 2025) or to support the 
work of the Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons in Syria. Moreover, the EU regime (like 
the US') contains some exemptions for dealings with the Syrian opposition (the Syrian National Coalition).

The EU global human rights sanctions regime 
On 7 December 2020, the Council set up the EU global human rights sanctions regime (Council 
Decision (CFSP) 2020/1999 and Council Regulation (EU) 2020/1998), which enabled the EU to 
target – through travel bans and asset freezes – individuals, entities and bodies responsible for, 
involved in or associated with serious human rights violations and abuses worldwide. Under this 
regime, one Syrian entity (the Syrian Republican Guard) and two individuals (the former Minister of 
Defence and the Chief of Staff of the Syrian Army) are listed. The regime itself applies until 
8 December 2026 and the listings until 8 December 2025. 
Restrictive measures against the proliferation and use of chemical weapons 
The EU restrictive measures against the proliferation and use of chemical weapons are based on 
Council Decision (CFSP) 2018/1544 and Council Regulation (EU) 2018/1542, adopted on 15 October 
2018. They introduce asset freezes and travel bans against persons and entities responsible for the 
development and use of chemical weapons, as well as those who provide financial, technical or 
material support, and those associated with all the above. Since September 2024, the list includes 
two entities: the Scientific Studies and Research Centre (SSRC, Syria's main entity in charge of 
developing chemical weapons), and five individuals (occupying various positions at the SSRC). 
Restrictive measures with respect to ISIL (Da'esh) and Al-Qaida 
The EU is obliged to implement all UNSC sanctions, including those related to ISIL (Da'esh) and Al-
Qaida. Accordingly, the EU has sanctioned HTS since 8 June 2018, in accordance with Council 
Regulation (EC) No 881/2002, after the UNSC Sanctions Committee listed Hayat Tahrir al-Sham as 
one of the Al-Nusrah Front's aliases. Both the Al-Nusrah Front and Abu Mohammad al-Jolani have 
been on the EU terror list since 2014, following their designation by the UN Sanctions Committee. 
Restrictive measures in relation to the 2005 terrorist bombing in Beirut 
Finally, the EU implements the UNSC sanctions in relation to the terrorist bombing in Beirut, 
Lebanon, on 14 February 2005.

Sanctions against Syria 
The United States maintains a broad regime of sanctions against Syria for its support of terrorism, 
interference in Lebanon, chemical weapons use, and, since 2011, human rights violations. The US 
designated Syria a state sponsor of international terrorism (SST) in December 1979. The main 
sanctions resulting from this designation include restrictions on US foreign assistance; a ban on 
defence exports and sales; certain controls over exports of dual-use items; and various financial and 
other restrictions. The US also imposes secondary sanctions on persons and countries engaging in

Sanctions imposed on Syria by other countries 
A number of European countries, such as North Macedonia, Moldova, Ukraine, Montenegro, Serbia, Albania, 
Bosnia and Herzegovina, Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland, have aligned with the EU 
sanctions on Syria. The UK's sanctions regime against Syria builds on the EU sanctions adopted at the time 
when it was still an EU Member State. Currently, the UK sanctions list includes the persons and entities 
designated under the Syria sanctions regime and the terrorist list relating to Al-Qaida and ISIL/Da'esh. 
Other countries, such as Australia, Canada, Turkey and Japan, have also imposed sanctions against Syria 
since 2011. That same year, the League of Arab States (LAS) imposed economic and financial sanctions and 
an arms embargo on Syria. After Syria's reinstatement to the Arab League in 2023, there was a gradual move 
towards normalisation of relations with Assad and a gradual lifting of sanctions. Today, the arms embargo 
appears to have been lifted and LAS is pushing for lifting international sanctions post-Assad.

The provision of humanitarian assistance by non-governmental organisations, the UN or the US government 
is authorised under certain conditions, based on authorisations, exemptions and general licences for 
humanitarian assistance. For example, the US' Syria General License 22, issued in 2022, allows for some 
commercial activities in northwest and northeast Syria in sectors such as agriculture, telecommunications, 
power grid infrastructure, construction, health, education, water and waste management, etc. It also 
authorises the purchase of refined oil of Syrian origin for use in Syria, and the processing or transfer of funds 
by third-country entities to or from Syria in support of the transactions authorised by the general licence. 
US counterterrorism sanctions 
In May 2014, the US added the Al-Nusrah Front to its designated entities and individuals lists: the 
Foreign Terrorist Organizations (FTO) list under the Immigration and Nationality Act, and the 
Specially Designated Global Terrorist list under Executive Order (E.O.) 13224 of September 2001. 
The entry was updated in May 2018 to designate other aliases, including HTS. Abu Mohammad al-
Jolani was listed in May 2013 and the US government set a US$10 million bounty on his head. The 
listing means that travel bans are instituted and all assets of designated individuals and FTOs subject 
to US jurisdiction are blocked; US individuals and entities are prohibited from engaging in any 
transactions with or providing material and other support to those designated. 
Lifting of international sanctions against Syria 
Various calls to ease sanctions 
After the fall of the Assad regime, Syria's new authorities have been appealing to Western 
governments to lift their sanctions, given that they targeted the Assad regime, and arguing that this 
is a key step to ensuring Syria's stability. As regards the terrorist designations of HTS, the group 
maintains that it is 'an independent entity that follows no organisation or party, al-Qaida or others'. 
Economic and humanitarian challenges in Syria 
Syria is currently facing significant economic challenges, including the need to rebuild severely damaged 
infrastructure, reduce the large current-account deficit, stem inflation and attract foreign investment. The 
estimated cost of reconstruction in Syria is at least US$250 billion, with a poverty rate of 90 % and the 
informal economy accounting for more than 60 % of economic activity. Syria's real GDP is now less than 
half of what it was before 2011, and further contraction is deemed likely in the short term. However, there 
is potential for recovery, especially if foreign support for reconstruction can be secured.8 The caretaker 
government is focused on urgent economic stabilisation, which includes setting up a hybrid monetary 
system to stabilise the currency and attract foreign aid and investment. Five key sectors are being 
prioritised: energy, telecoms, roads and airports, education and healthcare, to ensure basic services for the 
population. The caretaker government has also announced longer-term economic plans, specifically fiscal 
and monetary reforms. 
In addition to economic challenges, Syria is also facing one of the world's largest humanitarian crises. Over 
nearly 14 years of war, half a million people have been killed and more than 13 million people have been 
displaced internally and externally. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (UN 
OCHA) estimates that in 2024, 16.7 million people in Syria, or 70 % of the population, required assistance. 
There are still 7.2 million internally displaced people, many of whom are living in overcrowded camps, and 
1 million have been newly displaced within the country since the end of November 2024. Neighbouring 
countries such as Turkey, Lebanon, Jordan, Iraq and Egypt are hosting the majority of Syrians who have fled 
the country, while EU countries have granted refugee status to nearly 1.2 million Syrians. Syria also has one 
of the highest rates of unexploded ordnances in the world, which hinders the return of displaced persons 
and the delivery of aid. Out of the US$4.07 billion needed to address the humanitarian situation in Syria, 
only 34.2 % has been covered. Furthermore, the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR) is seeking US$310 million to 
address the critical needs of up to 1 million Syrian refugees expected to return to Syria between January 
and June 2025. While 200 000 Syrian refugees have already returned to Syria since Assad's fall, sanctions 
are an obstacle for the remaining 30 % of Syrian refugees who wish to return, according to the UNHCR.

Calls for easing sanctions date back to 2017, when HTS established its governance in Idlib. After 
forming the Syrian Salvation Government in November 2017, the group tried to portray itself as a 
local entity with a local agenda, not a global jihadi organisation. As humanitarian organisations found 
it difficult to work with the local authorities in Idlib due to their terrorist designation, and as HTS 
made efforts to improve relations with humanitarian aid groups, there was some hope that delisting 
the group would improve humanitarian aid to the region. Earlier calls for sanctions reform also aimed 
at reducing the sanctions' negative impact on the population. It was also reported that a few weeks 
prior to the regime's collapse, the Biden administration had been working on easing sanctions on 
Syria in exchange for Assad distancing himself from Iran and blocking Hezbollah arms shipments. 
After nearly 14 years of war, sanctions relief is considered essential to address the massive 
economic and humanitarian needs of the Syrian population, also aggravated by the impact of the 
international sanctions, resulting in widespread deindustrialisation and economic deterioration. 
According to a July 2024 UN study, unilateral sanctions have had unintended consequences on the 
Syrian population, affecting negatively the livelihoods of a majority of Syrians and their access to 
essential services in areas such as healthcare, education, food security, agriculture, water, sanitation 
and hygiene, and humanitarian assistance. Indirectly, even when exemptions exist, over-compliance 
undermines their effectiveness, affecting financial transactions, contracting, procurement, imports, 
and humanitarian operations. Two thirds of Syrians interviewed for the study expressed opposition 
to the sanctions, highlighting their adverse effects on their daily lives. 
Since 8 December 2024, various countries, including in the region (Türkiye, Egypt, Jordan, Lebanon, 
the Gulf Cooperation Council countries), some EU Member States, the Arab League, UN bodies,9 
human rights and humanitarian organisations have all called for the lifting of sanctions on Syria in 
order to support the country's economic recovery and facilitate the delivery of humanitarian aid and 
the return of refugees. Some Arab countries' and Türkiye's efforts to ease sanctions are driven by 
economic and strategic reasons, as they hope to enhance their own investment in post-Assad 
Syria. Besides other strategic interests in Syria, Türkiye also seeks the return of the 3 million 
Syrian refugees it hosts. For the West, including the EU, lifting sanctions remains conditional on the 
Syrian authorities leading an inclusive political transition process that safeguards the role of 
minorities and women and avoiding extremism.  
Experts continue to call on foreign states to engage with the new leaders in Damascus and 'steer 
them towards pragmatic, if not democratic, governance.' Others emphasise the international 
community's mistakes in Afghanistan, where foreign governments moved too slowly in easing 
sanctions and tackling the extreme humanitarian needs. As the new Syrian authorities have so far 
demonstrated a moderate governance approach and a conciliatory tone towards minorities, they 
argue that sanctions relief is necessary to end Syria's isolation and prevent the deepening of the 
economic and humanitarian crisis, which would have massive geopolitical repercussions for 
neighbouring and Western nations. In this context, Russia plays an important role in the US and the 
EU approaches towards Syria. Some analysts argue that any US sanctions waivers must ensure that 
Russia cannot take advantage of loopholes in the sanctions regime. Some EU Member States have 
also proposed linking sanctions relief to Russia leaving Syria.10 
A strong criticism against the EU and the US is that they have not proposed to the Syrian authorities 
a clear roadmap for sanctions relief, beyond the myriad of requirements on inclusivity, holding free 
and fair elections, and taking action against terrorism, without prioritising and clarifying what the 
West would offer in return. Other analysts argue that Western nations should at least clear the legal 
hurdles to aid groups and governments willing to invest in reconstruction and institution building. 
While sanctions were imposed to coerce the Assad regime, they are now being used as leverage for 
other purposes, state some Syrian experts, who worry that security will soon be affected unless the 
economic situation is swiftly addressed. They suggest other ways to pressure the new authorities, 
such as tying their recognition at the UN as Syria's legitimate government to the establishment of 
an inclusive interim government and fully lifting sanctions only afterwards. They further insist that 
concerns about HTS should not be a factor in maintaining sanctions on Syria (referring to the

be conditional on delivering inclusivity, effecting a democratic transition and denouncing terrorism. 
Ultimately, although the EU has a wide range of sanctions on Syria, it is the US sanctions that are 
considered the most stringent (also due to the role of the US dollar as the world's dominant reserve 
currency). Some suggest that the US administration should negotiate a meaningful arrangement 
with Syria's interim leaders, all the while removing swiftly and without conditions sanctions on critical 
sectors, and broadening exemptions to allow for commercial and economic activities. However, 
other analysts believe that broader delisting beyond short-term waivers – at the state, group, or 
personal levels – should be 'earned, not gifted'. 
Lifting of UN Security Council terrorist designations 
UN member states may at any time submit to the UNSC 1267 Sanctions Committee a request to 
delist an individual or group placed on the UNSC sanctions list. Such requests must include detailed 
information about the reasons why the designated persons or entities no longer meet the criteria, 
together with supporting evidence. The committee decides by consensus when the delisting request 
is made by a member state that did not propose the name for the listing. If the delisting request is 
made by the state that requested the listing, then the name is removed from the list after a fixed 
time, unless all fifteen members of the Committee decide to maintain the listing, or the matter is 
referred to the Security Council. Alternatively, individuals and entities on the sanctions list may 
address delisting requests to the Office of the Ombudsperson, with similar procedures as above. 
Some UN member states are reportedly discussing the possibility of making a delisting request for 
HTS and its leader al-Sharaa. Although the UN special envoy for Syria has suggested that delisting 
HTS is an option, there is yet no agreement among UNSC members. Russia, in particular, has been 
wary of the new Syrian rulers' jihadi links. Therefore, the prospect of divergence between UNSC 
members over timing and procedure is real. Nevertheless, some experts believe it is necessary to 
establish a framework for granting HTS some sanctions relief (e.g. travel ban exemptions for HTS 
officials), considering the UNSC concessions to the Taliban in Afghanistan since 2022. Setting 'clear 
and realistic benchmarks' for delisting would motivate HTS to cooperate, according to some 
analysts, particularly as the UNSC insists on the need to prevent ISIL (Da'esh) and other terrorist 
groups from re-establishing their capabilities in Syria. 
US sanctions relief 
On 6 January 2025, the US issued an order to ease restrictions (Syria General License (GL) 24), valid 
for six months, until 7 July 2025. Syria (GL) 24 builds on Syria General License 22 to expand 
authorisations for activities relating to providing essential services and continuity of governance 
functions across Syria. This includes the provision of electricity, energy, water, and sanitation. It lifts 
the ban on previously prohibited transactions with 'governing institutions' in Syria following 
8 December 2024; transactions in support of the sale, supply, storage, or donation of energy, 
including petroleum, petroleum products, natural gas and electricity to or within Syria; and the

The US president has a certain degree of flexibility in modifying, suspending or lifting sanctions on

lifted after the president certifies to Congress that Syria has met specific criteria or, alternatively, if 
the president invokes national security waivers included in the applicable law.

Reactions to the US waivers 
For many observers, the US waivers send a positive signal, making it easier to engage directly with 
the new Syrian administration and to provide humanitarian assistance. However, they will not have 
a significant impact, as the bulk of the US sanctions still apply, blocking entire sectors of the Syrian 
economy, the inflow of foreign investment into Syria, and trade with Syrian companies. The US has 
not removed restrictions on Syria's Central Bank, nor on assistance to Syria by international 
organisations. The waivers will help to ensure the delivery of basic services to the population, but 
will not enable the resumption of normal business activity, international trade and development 
assistance required to end Syria's isolation. Moreover, the time-limited waivers are not conducive to 
enhancing long-term recovery and investment, as their duration is insufficient for humanitarian 
players and businesses to establish a meaningful presence in Syria. Keeping HTS on the terrorist list 
raises concerns for humanitarian organisations, financial institutions or businesses that may have to 
engage with the new authorities, given the prohibition on offering material support to designated 
terrorist groups. In this context, such players may not risk investing in Syria. Conversely, other 
experts argue that, while the US should continue to provide short-term sanctions waivers and other 
measures to Syria in order to contribute to its reconstruction and to unhindered humanitarian aid 
flows, the broader listings should be maintained.

EU greenlight for gradual suspension of sanctions 
Soon after the ousting of the Assad regime, six Member States (Germany, France, the Netherlands, 
Spain, Finland and Denmark) called for easing the EU sanctions on Syria. Their proposal consists in 
facilitating civilian flights, reviewing sanctions on high-value goods, removing an export ban on oil 
and gas technology and reopening financial channels between the EU and Syria. In a speech before 
the European Parliament, on 19 December 2024, the European Commission President, 
Ursula von der Leyen, stated that '... we should also reconsider our sectoral sanctions, to facilitate 
reconstruction. ... to do so, we need to see real progress towards an inclusive political process.' The 
HR/VP, Kaja Kallas, stated that any easing of sanctions should be contingent upon the Syrian de 
facto authorities' approach to governing, which must be inclusive of different groups and women 
and should not allow for radicalisation. 
On 27 January 2025, the EU's Foreign Affairs Council reached political agreement on a roadmap for 
suspending sanctions against Syria, in a step-by-step approach. The first easing will involve 
sanctions that 'harm most the country's development', particularly in the energy, transport and 
financial sectors. The selection could still change as negotiations advance on technical aspects, 
which are expected to be finalised within weeks. Still to be decided are a timeline, the monitoring 
criteria and a possible snapback mechanism if EU conditions are not met. According to the HR/VP, 
the EU is prepared to move quickly with sanctions relief, regularly assessing the conditions in Syria 
for a further easing of sanctions, but is also ready to re-impose sanctions if necessary. The 
agreement therefore concerns a temporary suspension, contingent upon progress on the ground, 
such as the protection of all religious and ethnic minorities and respect for human rights and 
women's rights. The arms, dual-use items and surveillance equipment embargo, the restrictions on 
chemicals and the sanctions on persons and entities associated with the Assad regime remain in 
place. Regarding removing the HTS from the EU terrorist sanctions list, the EU's decision will follow 
that of the UNSC. The EU may also decide to impose conditions on the group's removal from the EU 
list to prevent risks relating to terrorist financing and Syria becoming a haven for terrorist groups. 
The suspension of sanctions will be effective once the necessary legal acts are adopted, amending 
the existing Council decision and Council regulation instituting the sanctions on Syria. Any revision 
of EU sanctions requires unanimous agreement among the 27 EU Member States. 
European Parliament 
Since 2011, Parliament has adopted numerous resolutions on the situation in Syria.12 In its most 
recent resolution on Syria of 28 February 2024, Parliament emphasised that restrictive measures 
must remain targeted, continue to ensure exemptions for humanitarian aid, and prioritise deliveries 
of aid at the local level. The Commission, the Council and the EU's external action service are asked 
to address the issue of financial over-compliance, which can prevent aid groups from transferring 
funds into Syria even when this is done to support activities exempt from sanctions. They are also 
urged to grant extensive and clear exemptions to trusted international humanitarian organisations. 
Parliament's President Roberta Metsola stated on 8 December 2024 that 'dialogue, unity, respect for 
fundamental rights, international law' must characterise the next steps. The same day, the 
presidents of the Committee on Foreign Affairs and of the delegation for relations with the Mashreq 
countries, together with the standing rapporteur for Syria, issued a statement calling for a peaceful 
transition that fulfils the democratic aspirations of the Syrian people and ensures the protection of 
all Syrians. On 17 December 2024, Parliament held a debate on Toppling of the Syrian regime, its 
geopolitical implications and the humanitarian situation in the region, during which several Members 
argued in favour of revising EU sanctions against Syria, taking into account the EU's priorities - fight 
against terrorism, destruction of chemical weapons, respect for neighbouring states, putting an end 
to the alliance with Russia and ensuring protection of minorities and women's rights. Conversely, 
some Members stated the EU should keep its sanctions in place, as long as there is no clear 
understanding of the events unfolding in Syria.
