Abstract
Messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccine is a promising candidate in cancer immunotherapy as it can encode tumor-associated antigens with an excellent safety profile. Unfortunately, the inherent instability of RNA and translational efficiency are major limitations of RNA vaccine. Here, we report an injectable hydrogel formed with graphene oxide (GO) and polyethylenimine (PEI), which can generate mRNA (ovalbumin, a model antigen) and adjuvants (R848)-laden nanovaccines for at least 30 days after subcutaneous injection. The released nanovaccines can protect the mRNA from degradation and confer targeted delivering capacity to lymph nodes. The data show that this transformable hydrogel can significantly increase the number of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells and subsequently inhibit the tumor growth with only one treatment. Meanwhile, this hydrogel can generate an antigen specific antibody in the serum which in turn prevents the occurrence of metastasis. Collectively, these results demonstrate the potential of the PEI-functionalized GO transformable hydrogel for effective cancer immunotherapy.
RNA-based vaccines have become an attractive strategy for cancer immunotherapy. (1,2) Cancer immunotherapy activates the dormant T-cells and helps the immune system in detecting or destroying tumor cells. (3) RNA vaccines are usually designed to translate tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and eventually trigger antigen specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which in turn eliminate or reduce tumor burden. (2,4) Unfortunately, the instinct instability and low efficiency of cellular uptake greatly limit the application of RNA vaccines. (5) In addition, the immunotherapy requires that the TAAs are expressed and presented at the right immunological tissue (i.e., lymph node). (1) Hence, it is crucial to develop effective technologies that can protect RNA vaccines from degradation and deliver them to proper tissue for translation. (6) Recently, nanoparticles have been applied to deliver RNA vaccines for cancer immunotherapy. (4,7) However, those vaccines may persist in delivering antigens for only 1–2 days after a single administration, which is much shorter than natural infections that expose antigens to the immune system for 1–2 weeks. (8) In order to achieve marked therapeutic outcomes, the nanoparticle-based vaccines need to be administered repeatedly which may generate unstable therapeutic outcomes and hinder its clinical transformation. Thus, there is an urgent need to establish an effective delivery system for long-term sustained release of RNA vaccines for cancer immunotherapy.
During cancer progression, the functions of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are disturbed and exhausted because of the immunosuppression or tolerance induction in the tumor microenvironment (TME). (1,9) Specifically, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells are considered as major drivers for antitumor immunity. (7) Therefore, stimulation and expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cell populations are crucial for cancer immunotherapy. However, many components in TME, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), macrophage type 2 (M2) cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) could make immunologic barriers against CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor responses. (10,11) To overcome this, it is important to stimulate the innate immune response to generate CD8+ T cells for durable and efficient antitumor immune responses. Immune adjuvants can enhance the immune response to the administrated antigens, and therefore have been exploited in the past several decades. (12) One widely used adjuvant is a toll-like receptor (TLR) agonist that can trigger the immune system and initiates innate and adaptive immune responses. (13,14) Nevertheless, the timing of antigen and adjuvant presentation to the immune system is still limited and it is challenging to achieve durable antitumor immune responses.
Hydrogel has been used for long-term releasing of encapsulated therapeutic agents. (15) The antigens or adjuvant can be released from hydrogel through concentration gradient or degradation of hydrogel. (16,17) However, this design feature may not be suitable for delivering RNA vaccines as the released free RNA vaccines cannot enter APCs efficiently and can be degraded in the cells/tissue quickly. In order to overcome this issue, we here reported an in situ transformable hydrogel for long-term release of RNA vaccine and adjuvant for cancer immunotherapy. Graphene oxide (GO) could provide the high drug-loading efficiency due to the large surface area and thereby has been widely investigated for drug delivery. (18,19) Resiquimod (R848) is a hydrophobic TLR7/8 agonist that can induce immune cell activation when used for cancer immunotherapy. (20,21) Herein, GO was designed to encapsulate the hydrophobic R848 through π–π stacking for in vivo delivery. Moreover, recent studies have indicated that functionalized GO can activate APC through a variety of TLR pathways. (22,23) Ovalbumin encoding mRNA (mOVA) was used as a model mRNA vaccine. Low molecular weight polyethylenimine (LPEI) was designed to bind mOVA through electrostatic interaction for translation. The reason that we used LPEI in this study is that regular PEI with high molecular weight is toxic to cells that significantly limit the potential application. (24,25) In contrast, LPEI is less toxic to cells but needs to improve its delivery and endo/lysosome escaping efficiency.
To address the above-mentioned challenges, as shown in Figure 1a, the GO and LPEI were mixed and fabricated with electrostatic interaction to form the injectable GO-LPEI hydrogel (GLP Gel). Meanwhile, the R848 and mOVA were encapsulated in the hydrogel through π–π stacking and electrostatic interaction. (26) Unlike traditional cross-linked (i.e., chemical reaction) hydrogel, the GLP-R848-mOVA Gel (GLP-RO Gel) is not stable at the interface when embedded in liquid solution and would transform to GLP-RO nanoparticles gradually (GLP-RO NPs, Figure 1a). The GLP-RO NPs can be slowly released from GLP-RO Gel after injected into subcutaneous layers, delivering R848 and mOVA to the lymph node. Finally, the GLP-RO NPs can translate OVA antigens in APCs for durable and efficient cancer immunotherapy (Figure 1a).
Results and Discussion
Characterizations of GLP-RO Gel and GLP-RO NPs
In order to obtain the transformable hydrogel, GO with a size of ∼200 nm (Figure S1) and LPEI with a molecular weight of 1.8 kDa were used in this study. The GO-to-LPEI feeding ratio was optimized to form the injectable hydrogel. As shown in Figure S2a,b, the stable GLP Gel can be obtained with the feeding mass ratio of 3.5:1 (GO/LPEI) and easily injected with a syringe. However, it is difficult to inject the GLP Gel from a syringe when increasing the feeding ratio to 3.75:1. Therefore, the feeding ratio of 3.5:1 was used for the following studies. Next, R848 or mOVA was mixed with GO or LPEI to build the GLP-RO Gel. The addition of R848 and mOVA has minimal effects on the hydrogel formation as shown in Figures 1b and S2b. The encapsulation efficiency of mOVA or R848 is ∼100% or 97%, which is attributed to the strong π–π stacking between GO and R848 or electrostatic interaction between LPEI and mOVA. The loading capacities are ∼1.24% and ∼5.99% for mOVA and R848, respectively. Typical scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of GLP-RO Gel are given in Figure 1c,d, showing the rough surface and layered structure. Rheological behaviors including elastic modulus and viscous modulus were investigated to confirm the strength or viscoelastic properties of the hydrogel. (27) Shear strain of 1% was in a stable range and selected to achieve linear deformation as shown in Figure S3a. Compared with the value of the viscous modulus, the elastic modulus maintained higher value regardless of frequency changes (Figure S3b), indicating the solid-like property of GLP-RO Gel.
The transforming capability of GLP-RO Gel was investigated by adding phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) on the surface of the hydrogel. As shown in Figure S4, the volume of GLP-RO Gel was continuously decreased and almost disappeared after 30 days at room temperature, whereas the volume of PBS solution was increased and became darker. To confirm the formation of GLP-RO NPs, the nanoparticles in PBS solution were collected for SEM and AFM imaging as shown in Figure 1e,f, suggesting the successful formation of GLP-RO NPs with a uniform size and spherical morphology. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements further confirmed the uniform distribution of GLP-RO NPs with an average size of ∼220 nm (Figure 1g). The successful formation of GLP-RO NPs was further investigated for their surface potential. The zeta potential of GLP-RO NPs is ∼−0.52 mV, which is significantly different with mOVA-laden LPEI (LPEI-mOVA, ∼16.37 mV) or R848-laden GO (GO-R848, ∼−29.8 mV, Figure 1h). Those results suggest that LPEI-mOVA (positive) may bind with GO-R848 (negative) through electrostatic interaction to form the GLP-RO NPs. Moreover, the in vitro drug releases were almost similar for both drugs, suggesting both drugs were encapsulated together in nanoparticles (Figure 1i). It is also worth noting that the transforming process mainly occurred at the liquid–hydrogel interface (Figure S4), suggesting the liquid dilution might disturb the connection of hydrogel.
We next investigated the capability of the GLP-RO NPs in protecting the RNA vaccines. As shown in Figure S5a, migration of mOVA into the electrophoretic gel was prevented in GLP-O (without R848) or GLP-RO NPs compared to free mOVA. The stability of the mOVA in GLP NPs was examined by incubating in serum. Free mOVA disappeared in less than 6 h because of the degradation. In contrast, the mOVA in GLP-RO NPs was detectable for at least 48 h, confirming that the GLP-RO NPs could protect the mOVA from degradation in serum (Figure 1j). The stability of mOVA in GLP-RO Gel is also important for long-term releasing. As shown in Figure S5b, the mOVA can be encapsulated and no free mOVA were released from GLP-RO Gel after 10 days. Importantly, after replaced with heparin sodium the mOVA in GLP-RO Gel shows a similar band as compared with fresh mOVA, suggesting the mOVA is not degraded in gel (Figure S5c).
In vitro Translation and Activation of APCs for Antigen Presentation
To confirm the antigen presentation capability, cellular uptake of GLP-RO NPs was first investigated. As shown in Figure S6a,b, GLP, GLP-O, or GLP-RO NPs can efficiently enter the APCs (i.e., RAW264.7 or DC2.4 cells) after labeled LEPI with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). The fluorescence in PBS- or free drug- (R848-mOVA) treated cells is minimal. Confocal images show that most of the GLP-RO NPs were merged with the red staining of late endosomes/lysosomes, suggesting the RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells may uptake the nanoparticles through endocytosis (Figure S6c). (28)
Next, we investigated the capability of endosomes/lysosomes escaping GLP-RO NPs by labeling mOVA with Cy5.5. Similarly, flow cytometry results indicate that the fluorescence intensity is significantly higher for GLP-RO NP-treated RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells compared with other control groups (Figure 2a,b and Figure S7). It is worth noting that the fluorescence intensity of the GLP-RO NPs’ group was stronger than GLP or the GLP-O NPs’ treated cells, which might because R848 can change the surface potential and then affect the cellular uptake. Importantly, some red fluorescence is not merged with LysoTracker (green) shown in confocal images, indicating that GLP-RO NPs can help mOVA escaping from lysosomes and distributing in cytosol (Figure 2c). In contrast, there is almost no red fluorescence in free mOVA-treated cells (Figure S8). This is not surprising as PEI has the “proton sponge effect” to break the integrity of lysosomes. (29) This capacity was further confirmed with green fluorescent protein (GFP) encoding mRNA (mGFP). Indeed, as shown in Figure S9, strong green fluorescence can be obviously observed in mGFP-laden GLP (GLP-G) or mGFP and R848-laden GLP (GLP-RG) NPs treated cells. Cell viability studies indicate that the cytotoxicity of GLP-O or GLP-RO NPs was similar to free LPEI at different concentrations, showing that the formation of GLP NPs do not increase the cytotoxicity of LPEI and the cytotoxicity of R848-GO to both RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells is also minimal (Figure S10).
The expression of OVA in APCs was then confirmed with Western blot and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Western blot data show that the OVA proteins were highly expressed in GLP-O and GLP-RO NP treated RAW264.7 and DC2.4 cells (Figure 2d). Similarly, the absorbance of OVA in GLP-O and GLP-RO NP treated cell groups was significantly increased according to the ELISA results (Figure 2e,f). Of note, the expression of OVA in GLP-RO NP treated cells was higher than other groups, which is probably due to the enhanced cellular delivery (Figure 2a-b).
Meanwhile, we noticed that many of the RAW264.7 and DC2.4 were activated during the incubation with GLP-RO NPs (Figure S11). To confirm this, the activation of DC2.4 cells was investigated by detecting the CD80 positive (the hallmark of activated dendritic cells) cells in the whole population selected with CD11c. (30) As shown in Figure 2g,h, flow cytometry results show that GLP-RO NPs can effectively activate the DC2.4 cells compared with other groups. Similarly, the percentage of CD86 positive cells was also significantly increased after being treated with GLP-RO NPs (Figure S12). More importantly, the presentation of OVA peptide on MHC class I was further confirmed by detecting SIINFEKL-H2Kb, which is the complex of OVA-derived CD8+T epitope and MHC class I molecules. (31) Significant antigen-presentation of the OVA peptide SIINFEKL was observed in GLP-RO NP treated dendritic cells (Figure 2g,i). Collectively, those results indicate that the released GLP-RO NPs can efficiently deliver the mRNA and adjuvants to APCs and activate them for antigen presentation.
In vivo Long-Term Release of Antigens and Adjuvants
The long-term releases of mOVA and R848 were studied by subcutaneously injecting GLP-RO Gel in C57BL/6 mice. Ideally, the hydrogel could continually release GLP-RO NPs in which vaccines (both antigens and adjuvants) can be delivered to lymph node for immunotherapy (Figure 3a). To confirm the capability for long-term release, subcutaneously injected GLP, GLP-O, and GLP-RO Gels were collected on days 10, 20, and 30. As shown in Figure 3b-c, the sizes and weights of all the hydrogels were gradually decreased and the degradation process lasted for at least 30 days, indicating the capacity of long-term releasing. Of note, the weight of GLP Gel is not obviously decreased on day 10, which might because the LPEI can absorb the negative materials in tissue. This is not observed in GLP-O or GLP-RO Gels as the LPEI has already bound with mOVA.
Importantly, the existence of mOVA in the hydrogel was confirmed with gel electrophoresis. The image indicates that migration of mOVA into the gel was almost completely prevented, suggesting most, if not all mOVA should be continuously encapsulated in GLP-RO Gel 30 days after injection (Figure S13). The mOVA was then replaced with heparin sodium for quantitative measurements. The amounts of mOVA in GLP-O and GLP-RO Gels were continually decreased and not affected by the R848 (Figure 3d). More than 95% of mOVA were released from GLP-RO Gel on day 30. This is consistent with the in vitro images as shown in Figure S4. There is almost nothing observable in the tube after nanoparticle transformation.
Next, the GLP, GLP-O, and GLP-RO Gels collected from mice were sliced for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Interestingly, H&E images on days 10 and 30 exhibit that many cells were grew in the edge of hydrogels, probably due to the minimal cytotoxicity of GLP Gel (Figure 3e). It is worth noting that almost no cells can be observed in interior of the GLP-O and GLP-RO Gels, which is probably because the Gels are stable and their interior porosity is not enough for cell migration (Figure 3e). This is consistent with in vitro results that the transformation of GLP-RO Gel only occurred at the liquid-hydrogel interface (Figure S4). To confirm the released GLP-RO NPs can migrate to lymph nodes in vivo, C57BL/6 mice were treated with PBS, Cy5.5 labeled free mOVA or GLP-RO Gel through subcutaneous injection. The lymph nodes were collected for imaging after 2 days. The fluorescence intensity was significantly increased in the lymph nodes from GLP-RO Gel treated mice compared with free mOVA (Figure 3f-g), indicating the released GLP-RO NPs can deliver mOVA and R848 to lymph nodes for immunotherapy. The imaging of major organs shows the distribution of free mOVA or GLP-RO Gel is minimal in heart, lung, spleen, and kidney whereas the fluorescence is detectable in liver (Figure S14).
In vivo tumor immunotherapy
Having confirmed the long-term release of mOVA and R848, we next investigated the antitumor capability of GLP-RO Gel using a B16-OVA melanoma model (Figure 4a). The mice were treated with GLP-RO Gel only one time on day 4 after implanted B16-OVA tumor cells (day 0, Figure 4b). The immunotherapeutic efficacy of different treatments, indicated with tumor growth inhibition, was assessed as shown in Figure 4c and Figure S15. The GLP Gel had minimal effects on tumor growth compared with the saline group. Free drugs (R848-mOVA) or GLP-O Gel can inhibit the growth of B16-OVA melanoma tumors to a certain degree. Importantly, the GLP-RO Gel exhibited the highest therapeutic efficacy, which might due to the integration of better antigen presentation, targeted lymph node delivery, and long-term release capability (Figures 2 and 3). The size and weight of the tumors harvested from the GLP-RO Gel group on day 22 were significantly less than that from other groups (Figure 4d,e). Moreover, H&E images revealed extensive necrosis in the tumors from the GLP-RO Gel group whereas tumors from all of the other groups were more viable (Figure 4f).
For analysis of immune response, we assessed CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells in the treated mice as which are the typical indicators of cellular immune response and play an important role for inhibiting tumor growth. (4) Indeed, CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells significantly increased in the GLP-RO Gel group compared with other groups (Figure 4g and Figure S16). Moreover, the Saline, R848-mOVA, and GLP Gel groups showed negligible tumor infiltration of CD8+T cells (Figure S17), whereas GLP-O Gel increased the frequency of intratumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells and GLP-RO Gel exhibited highest numbers. Meanwhile, the levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were detected in the serum in various treatment groups, and the results showed that the level of TNF-α in the GLP-RO Gel group was significantly higher than other groups (Figure 4h), which is probably because R848 and GO could activate immune cells. Interestingly, the GLP-RO Gel treatment could induce the highest level of OVA-specific antibodies in the serum compared with others (Figure 4i), suggesting GLP-RO Gel can not only inhibit the growth of tumors but also prevent the tumor recurrence or metastasis formation. The body weights of mice were stable during the 22 days of observation (Figure S18a), probably because the nanomaterials used in this study are more biocompatible (Figure 3e). Moreover, there is no evident damage to major organs collected from mice in the GLP-RO Gel group compared with the saline group, according to the H&E images (Figure S18b).
In Vivo Prevention of Metastasis
As mentioned above, OVA-specific antibodies can be generated in the serum after the GLP-RO Gel treatment. We then investigated the antimetastasis efficacy of GLP-RO Gel (Figure 5a). As shown in Figure 5b for the lung images, vaccination with the GLP-RO Gel could induce a potent antimetastasis effect, significantly preventing the formation of lung metastases. This is further confirmed with the lung weights and tumor indexes (Figure 5c,d). H&E staining indicates that intensive metastases are observed in the saline or GLP Gel treated mice but can be partially alleviated after treatment with R848-mOVA or GLP-O Gel (Figure 5e). Strikingly, there were no observable metastases in the lung tissues after only one immunization of GLP-RO Gel, confirming the durable antitumor capability of GLP-RO Gel.
Further immune analysis shows that the GLP-RO Gel treatment exhibited the highest levels of CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells and TNF-α, compared with other groups (Figure 5f,g and Figure S19). Importantly, only GLP-O and GLP-RO Gel groups could generate OVA-specific antibodies in the serum, and R848 (for GLP-RO Gel) could further increase the levels of the specific antibodies (Figure 5h), which might result in the better therapeutic efficacy. As the antigen presenting dendritic cells could be proliferated and differentiated into effector and memory T cells after stimulation, the immunological memory may also help to prevent the metastasis. We also noticed that the body weights of mice do not change during all the treatments (Figure S18c).
Conclusion
In summary, a transformable hydrogel (GLP-RO Gel) has been developed to release mRNA (mOVA) and adjuvant (R848)-laden nanoparticles for long-term cancer immunotherapy. This transformable hydrogel can not only encapsulate and protect mRNA from degradation but also target lymph nodes to activate immune cells. After injected subcutaneously, GLP-RO Gel could release GLP-RO NPs for at least for 30 days without obvious side effects. Importantly, the mOVA in GLP-RO Gel is still detectable after injected in vivo for 30 days. As a result, the GLP-RO Gel can efficiently inhibit the tumor growth with only one treatment. mOVA and R848 in GLP hydrogel could efficiently activate the immune system and generate tumor antigen antibodies in the serum and thereby effectively prevent the metastasis formation. Collectively, the present study demonstrates the great potential of the GLP-RO Gel in achieving durable and efficient cancer immunotherapy.
