'Caught in Providence' scores daytime Emmy nomination
Ask Judge Frank Caprio what it’s like to see his show score a daytime Emmy nomination, and he’ll quickly correct you.
“It’s not a show. It’s a court proceeding.”
That’s right. “Caught in Providence” is a real-life, televised series of cases, from parking tickets to speeding violations. It’s Caprio’s approach that has turned those everyday interactions into entertainment gold.
He sums up his philosophy in three words: respect, dignity and compassion. Or, more simply put, what his parents taught him.
“That’s how we were brought up,” Caprio said. “We were brought up to help people, to treat them with respect and compassion and understanding, which is what my parents did. I just do that on the bench, and it has resonated throughout the world.”
Part of that help came from a defendant who wanted to lend a hand to another in need.
“She couldn’t afford to pay her fine and I gave her time to pay it,” Caprio recalled. “The fellow who came after her said, ‘Judge, I’d like to pay the fine for this woman because her situation is so compelling,’ and that was televised. As a result of that, we began getting people sending in money from all over the country, all over the world actually, to help people pay their fines.”
Now, those funds are used to assist others in paying fines, a hallmark of Caprio’s humanitarian courtroom.
The program’s journey to its Emmy nomination in the outstanding courtroom program category was a long road reaching from more than two decades on local TV to national syndication in 2018.
And with Caprio’s brother Joseph serving as the executive producer, creating it was a family affair. The idea began with Joseph’s sister-in-law, Joyce. At the time, Joseph was filming a mix of government events, convention center happenings and sports games, so Joyce asked him a question.
“She says, ‘Joey, you’re filming all these these things, why don’t you film your brother?'” he remembered.
Upon hearing the idea, Caprio wasn’t sure anyone would buy it.
“Joey, if you want to do it, you’re welcome to do it,” Joseph recalled his brother saying. “But I don’t know how many people would be really interested in parking tickets.”
As it turns out, a lot of people were.
In the program’s early stages, Joseph set up his personal phone as a comment line for viewers who wanted to call in with feedback, which ran the gamut from compliments to contempt.
“It started to fill up two times a day, three times a day,” Joseph said. “Then we got a second line, and that was filling up, and then a third line.”
That audience has since grown from Rhode Islanders to those in states across the country and even overseas.
David, Caprio’s son and another producer on the program, said the reason is his father’s approach.
“It’s people's idea of what a court proceeding should be, where the common person without a lawyer has the chance to either win or to be treated fairly when it’s just them against the judge or them against the system, which is what the courtroom represents there.”
For Caprio, fostering a just courtroom is just his way.
“I’m not a prosecutor on the bench,” he said. “I don’t wear a badge under my robe. I wear a heart.”
