Updated July 22, 2026
In recent years, heightened scrutiny has targeted the integrity of election processes, particularly regarding noncitizen participation in U.S. elections. We address here four frequently asked questions concerning noncitizen voting in American elections:
- Can noncitizens vote?
- Have there been documented cases of noncitizen voting?
- How do election offices verify voters’ citizenship status?
- What are the penalties for voting as a noncitizen?
Can noncitizens vote?
The Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 explicitly prohibits noncitizens from voting in federal elections. It is not legal in any state for a noncitizen to cast a ballot in an election for federal office. While federal law does not prohibit noncitizens from casting a ballot in state or local elections, no state currently allows noncitizens to vote in statewide elections. Municipalities in three states—California, Maryland, and Vermont—and Washington, DC allow noncitizens to vote in certain local elections.1
- San Francisco allows resident noncitizen parents and guardians to vote in school board elections.
- Oakland passed a ballot measure in 2022 also giving noncitizen parents and legal guardians the ability to vote in school board elections. The measure is still pending implementation.
- Several municipalities in Maryland and Vermont permit noncitizens to vote in municipal elections.
- Washington, DC, began allowing noncitizen residents to vote in non-federal elections in 2022.
Election offices in communities that allow noncitizens to vote in local elections maintain processes to ensure that a registered noncitizen voter cannot vote for state or federal races. Maryland’s cities, for example, maintain separate ballots for municipal elections. This ensures that noncitizens cannot receive a ballot for state or federal contests.
Have there been documented cases of noncitizens voting?
Illegal voting, including by noncitizens, is routinely investigated and prosecuted by the appropriate authorities, and there is no evidence that noncitizen voting has ever been significant enough to impact an election’s outcome.
When investigating noncitizen voting, it is important to distinguish between voter registration and casting a ballot. Noncitizen registration is already rare, and noncitizen voting is even less common.
Recent public debate has focused heavily on audits of voter registration lists, as these audits sometimes flag individuals who appear to be registered as noncitizens. In many cases, however, further review shows the individual is in fact a citizen, and they were only flagged due to clerical errors or outdated records. For example:
- Ohio Secretary of State Frank LaRose flagged 521 cases of potential noncitizen voting between 2019 and September 2023. But an Ohio Capital Journal review found just one case in which a noncitizen was ultimately charged with voter fraud. The review noted that this was due to a combination of accidental registration and a lag in how the department of motor vehicles updates an individual’s citizenship status after naturalization.
- Utah performed a citizenship review of its entire voter registration list from April 2025–May 2026. After a time-intensive, multi-step review of more than 2 million registered voters, they identified only 27 confirmed instances of noncitizen registration.2
- Georgia: In October 2024, Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger announced that a citizenship audit of 8.2 million registered voters identified 20 noncitizens who had registered to vote. Of those 20 individuals, just nine had cast ballots in prior elections, most before Georgia implemented enhanced citizenship verification through the Department of Driver Services in 2012. The cases were referred to local prosecutors for investigation.
Nationwide, records from a federal citizenship verification tool show that just 0.04% of voter verification cases are returned as noncitizens.
In the rare instances in which noncitizens have been registered to vote—or have voted—the fault usually lies in administrative error. For example:
- Oregon: Oregon officials in 2024 revealed that a clerical error caused 1,940 individuals to be added to the voter rolls, an unknown number of whom may have been noncitizens. Due to a poorly designed computer program, Department of Motor Vehicle workers mistakenly affirmed that these individuals had provided proof of citizenship when, in fact, they had not. Although it is possible that some portion of these 1,940 individuals were noncitizens, many were later found to be citizens. Nevertheless, given that only 44 of the 1,940 later voted, the clerical error could not have affected the outcome of any elections in a state that, in 2024, cast more than 2.3 million ballots.
- Texas: Recently disclosed internal communications between former Texas Secretary of State Jane Nelson and U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services reveal that the state mistakenly registered noncitizens to vote, even though these individuals had acknowledged their noncitizen status on a voter registration application.
- New Jersey: On July 21, 2026, New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill announced that a software glitch in the state’s Motor Vehicle System caused approximately 6,600 ineligible noncitizens to be registered to vote, even though those individuals reported that they were not citizens during the registration transaction. The governor acknowledged that roughly 400 of these individuals cast ballots. However, there is no evidence to date that these votes altered the outcome of any races in New Jersey—a state that, in 2024, recorded over 4 million cast ballots.
While federal law prohibits voting by noncitizens in federal elections, prosecution typically requires demonstrating that the individual knowingly violated federal law. Therefore, the cases outlined above—of ineligible noncitizens being registered to vote through no fault of their own, and often without even their knowledge—are best addressed through administrative improvements. Such improvements, which can be found in a prior BPC report, include investing in modern motor vehicle department and state election office technology and infrastructure; strengthening coordination and data-sharing between motor vehicle departments and voter registration officials; and expanding back-end verification processes, which use existing government data to verify the citizenship statuses of registered voters.
How do election offices verify voters’ citizenship status?
The National Voter Registration Act of 1993 requires that individuals registering to vote anywhere in the United States attest under penalty of perjury that they are a U.S. citizen.
Depending on the state, election offices then employ a combination of other tools to further verify status:
- Review of documents that an individual used to obtain a state-issued ID or driver’s license. In states using this model, the motor vehicle department shares the types of documents a voter used to obtain an ID with the state or local election office. Those documents, such as a birth certificate or passport, can often confirm a voter’s citizenship. When they don’t, the election office can do additional investigation.3
- Use of federal data to verify citizenship. 23 states have an agreement with the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services SAVE System to verify voters’ citizenship status.
- Use of local and state data to verify citizenship. Election officials in some states also consult jury list dismissals when the reason for dismissal is a lack of citizenship status.
For more on citizenship verification best practices, read BPC’s report, Modernizing Voter List Maintenance: An Evidence-Based Framework for Access and Integrity.
What are penalties for voting as a noncitizen? Does voting by a noncitizen affect their immigration status?
There are significant penalties for registering or voting as a noncitizen. Illegally voting in a federal election can result in a fine and up to one year in federal prison, and noncitizens found guilty of this crime may also face deportation and revocation of their legal status under immigration law. Falsely claiming U.S. citizenship for the purpose of registering to vote may also result in deportation or denial of future immigration status, even if no voting occurs.
The enormity of these penalties means that deterrence is an important factor in ensuring compliance with laws that prohibit noncitizens from voting in federal elections. The risk of deportation, jail time, and fines often deter intentional efforts by noncitizens to register and vote.
