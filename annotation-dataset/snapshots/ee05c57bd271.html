Alysa Liu is now the best chance the United States has to win the country’s first women’s singles figure skating medal in two decades. Sitting in third place after the short program — with Isabeau Levito in 8th and Amber Glenn in 13th — she carries the hopes of Team USA into the free skate.
Four years ago, she also carried the hopes of the LGBTQ community. No, Liu hadn’t come out or said she was part of the community. However, on social media she had expressed support for the LGBTQ community and Pride.
She’d even listed her pronouns on Instagram as “she/they.” That prompted many people to say she was “nonbinary” or “queer.”
Yet today, those posts all seem to be gone. Liu might be LGBTQ. She might not be. Either way, she’s referred to in many places as “alt” — living her life how she wants.
Still, her Instagram profile now doesn’t share any of the seeming support she offered four years ago for the LGBTQ community, that Outsports could find. That doesn’t mean her private support has changed — There seems to be no way this young woman has changed her opinion on support for the gays.

In addition, her Instagram profile during the Beijing Olympics listed “they” as a pronoun. Any reference to her preferred pronouns has also been removed. Her official US Figure Skating profile lists her pronouns as “she/her.”
It’s impossible to understand motive for anyone who might publicly use certain pronouns and changes them, or publicly supports LGBTQ Pride then erases it. People make clean breaks from social media all the time — though Liu still displays active posts from before 2022, when these were available.
Young adults can experiment with certain pronouns and change. This can be an organic part of their personal journey.
Alysa Liu has been known for her individual self-expression. She has dyed her hair differently as a reflection of that, even changing her style during the current season from a longer cut to shorter. She’s kept some blonde strips in her hair throughout.
Alysa Liu shines on the ice
Alysa Liu has had a lot of success skating on the ice. At the previous Beijing Winter Olympics in 2022, she finished sixth overall.
She won two United States National Championships a few years ago, finishing second to Amber Glenn the last two seasons. She won the World Championship last season, and this season she won gold in the Grand Prix Final.
After the women’s singles short program at these Olympics, Liu sits in third place, just 2.12 points behind Ami Nakai of Japan, with Japan’s Kaori Sakamoto in second. Surrounded by the Japanese in the current standings, Liu is trailed by 2.59 points by Mone Chiba.
At the Grand Prix Final a couple months ago, Liu’s third-place in the free skate was enough to jump her from second to first. If she can nail a top-three free skate on Friday, something strange would have to happen for her to miss the medal podium.
We’ll be cheering for Liu to succeed in the free skate at these Olympics, hoping for Team USA to win that women’s figure skating medal that has eluded them for so long.
And we’re sure Liu’s support for the community hasn’t changed. People — particularly young people — shift how they approach social media, and we’re hopeful that Liu is still there for her LGBTQ friends and family.
