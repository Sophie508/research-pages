It’s called the “Hygiene Hypothesis” and it’s not so much the eosinophils, it’s one branch of the immune system (Th2) that has nothing to do so it turns on the self. The Th2 response is wholly dedicated to metazoan parasites (i.e.; protozoa and parasitic worms). Gut mircoflora are also included in the hypothesis but that’s a different immune process.
And the intentional infections are done in a clinical setting using parasites of pigs/dogs (hookworms) to alleviate GI autoimmune diseases such a as Chrone’s disease and extreme IBS. But not in the US. Because the parasites are in the wrong host, they do not reproduce so the patients have to get reinfected every few months. The results are actually far superior to the traditional medicines and treatments. Pretty cool stuff.
I’m sure some people infect themselves with parasites but that’s not advisable unless you know what you’re doing.

I think a lot of that is, well, horse-hockey.
You don't have more autonomy in a small tribe, as you have to participate in doing things for the tribe, or you'll likely be kicked out. Especially if you don't "fit in". You don't have more freedom. In fact, you can have less. A lot less. Things are more interpersonal, and if you piss off the big boss, you're going to have to face some consequences. That limits what you can say and do.
You don't have more free-time because things take a lot longer to accomplish. I think the mistake here is people sitting around a fire while doing things like processing sinew (you chew it) and other things that don't look necessarily like work to anthropologists, but are actually necessary tasks. Things like tending a fire while smoking meat to preserve it (a process that takes at least 24 hours). Doesn't look like work, but it is. If you don't do it, you ruin much of your meat.
As for wage slavery, you still end up working for someone, unless you're the chief. You have to do X, Y, and Z, or you're going to be exiled from the group for non-participation, a drag on resources but without any actual benefit. You're as much a slave to the tribe as you are to the company. But at least the company only has you 8 hours a day.
People have this sort of Rousseauian noble savage concept of how things were better back in the days of roving bands of hunter/gatherers, and nothing could be further from the truth. Without the inherent legal and social protections we've built up over the millennia to moderate the instinct of "might makes right", life back then was almost certainly worse for the majority from a social standpoint.
Life was, as the saying goes, "nasty, brutish, and short".
But don't take my word for it. Go ahead and learn some primitive skills and go camping using only those skills. See how well you do. Trust me, you'll learn a lot.
The following excerpt is from Time Magazine, 1969. Anthropology: The Original Affluent Society. https://time.com/archive/6637219/anthropology-the-original-affluent-society/
[Imagine a society in which the work week seldom exceeds 19 hours, material wealth is considered a burden, and no one is much richer than anyone else. Unemployment is high there, sometimes reaching 40%—not because the society is shiftless, but because it believes that only the able-bodied should work, and then no more than necessary. In most hunter-gatherer societies, you have more autonomy, free time, and freedom because you don't have to work 40 hrs a week to survive. The food gathering part takes up significantly less time than that, depending on where you live. The day to day tasks include weaving nets or baskets, cooking/curing food, tanning hides, making clothing, tending fires, etc. These tasks are done with the group voluntarily. These are the types of activities many people find interesting and fun to do anyway. Food and other resources are distributed freely because food goes bad if no one eats it, and because the stronger the group is, the more resilient everyone becomes.
People tell stories, sing songs, talk, or process their thoughts while doing the necessary tedious tasks. You pull your weight because you want to, not because of coercion, threats, or manipulation. No one is holding a gun to your head if you don't comply. You do the tasks because you naturally want to support the group which is immediately supporting your existence. Their lifestyles are far from the misleading "nasty, brutish, short" descriptions.
I recommend you visit a primitive skills gathering or go spend time with some existing indigenous tribes. They are some of the most egalitarian and kind people you may ever meet. See how well you do. Trust me, you'll learn a lot.
Umm, I've done both.
I've actually gone out into the woods myself. I've learned the primitive skills. I can start a fire by friction. I can knap stone tools. As a teen I hunted squirrels with a blowgun, and rabbits with a throwing stick. Around the year 2000, I switched to all primitive hunting: Flintlock long rifle for gun season, and a copy of the Subury bow for archery season, with arrows I made myself.
I know that there is plenty of work to be done when living primitive, and in fact, so much work that you can't survive long-term in a temperate environment on your own.
This is why Ishi, last of the Yahi, was starving to death less than 3 years after being left on his own. There is just too much work for a single person. And he was born into that lifestyle, and lived it for something like 50 years.
But once his uncle and sister were driven off/killed, and his elderly mother died, he simply couldn't do all the things necessary to keep himself fed. Not in an area where winters are a thing*.
I think a good analogy to this is the chef in the kitchen, making a soup. Only a small amount of his time is actually cutting up vegetables, meat, etc. and adding it to the soup. Most of the time, if he's not cooking something else, is just keeping an eye to make sure the pot isn't boiling over, and giving it a stir, maybe tasting it. Mostly just standing around. BUT THAT'S NOT FREE TIME. He's still got to keep an eye on it. He can't go play canasta with his chef buddies. He's still working.
I think the same basic thing is in play with primitive tasks, and preserving meat or fish by smoking it is a perfect example. It takes some time to cut the meat or fish up to the proper size, and to make the rack for smoking it, but then it's mostly just tending the fire to make sure it's the right size and adequately smokey (using green wood). That doesn't look like work to modern eyes, but if you just walk away to go to sleep and don't have someone else monitoring it, it's going to rot.
That's still work, but to modern eyes it looks like guys around a campfire jaw-jacking, and not actually working. But it's something that you have to do and you can't stop doing it.
There are a whole bunch of time consuming tasks that aren't really nose-to-the-grindstone work (though there is enough of that too: Try brain tanning a hide sometime). Things that seem like leisure time to people unfamiliar with that lifestyle.
I don't know why this concept is so hard to grasp.
*You can do it in a place with a year-round growing season, which is why Alexander Selkirk, marooned on what is now called Robinson Crusoe Island, was able to last 4 years instead of 3 years, and in much better health than Ishi.

https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/medieval-monks-were-riddled-with-worms-study-finds
The researchers tested 19 monks from the friary grounds and 25 locals from All Saints cemetery, and found that 11 of the friars (58%) were infected by worms, compared with just eight of the general townspeople (32%).
They say these rates are likely the minimum, and that actual numbers of infections would have been higher, but some traces of worm eggs in the pelvic sediment would have been destroyed over time by fungi and insects.
The 32% prevalence of parasites among townspeople is in line with studies of medieval burials in other European countries, suggesting this is not particularly low – but rather the infection rates in the monastery were remarkably high.
And a study on Sagalassos (an ancient Roman era city) that actually noticed pretty low rates of parasitic worm infection compared to other Roman sites likely due to massive use of anthelmintic plants by the locals:
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/320196425_Intestinal_parasites_from_the_2nd-5th_century_AD_latrine_in_the_Roman_baths_at_Sagalassos_Turkey

The answers here are almost all part of the whole story but that's a gigantic question, and it has an answer that's too long to fully give here.
Briefly, yes people lived with and died from parasites at all times throughout history. However, herbal and other medicinal remedies, cultural and religious food laws, cooking methods, living conditions, knowledge about food animals, and more had a huge impact on the prevalence and treatment of parasitic disease. Early humans, Neanderthals, and probably all other close human relatives co-evolved with medicinal plants that specifically treat parasitic disease.
So the answer isn't as simple as, "they died." Many did, but that's a severely oversimplified answer. A better answer is that traditional diets and medicines were used to prevent and treat parasitic disease, and were successful to a degree.
